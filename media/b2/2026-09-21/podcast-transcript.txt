Our first word is tutor. A tutor is a private teacher who works with you one on one, usually outside of regular school hours.
A tutor is different from a classroom teacher. A classroom teacher has thirty students, but a tutor has just one: you.
My cousin worked with a math tutor all through high school, and her grades went up fast. The tutor found her weak spots in the first week.
That's the magic of a good tutor — they notice what you hide in a big class. I once hired an English tutor before a big exam, and the tutor made me speak out loud every day until I stopped being shy.
So if you feel stuck, a tutor might be the answer. Ask around, read reviews, and try one session with a tutor before you decide.
Next up is revise, and listeners, this one is huge in British English. To revise means to study something again before a test.
Right — in American English people often say review, but revise is the classic word here. If your exam is on Friday, you revise your notes from Monday to Thursday.
I like to revise in small pieces. Twenty minutes, a short break, then revise again. Cramming everything the night before never works for me.
Totally agree. When you revise actively — testing yourself instead of just rereading — your memory holds on much longer.
So the takeaway is simple: revise a little, revise often, and revise before you feel ready.

Word number three is comprehension. Comprehension is the ability to understand what you read or hear. Not just recognizing the words — truly getting the meaning.
That's why teachers give reading comprehension tests. The comprehension section asks you questions about a story or an article.
Exactly. You can know every word in a paragraph and still miss the point. Real comprehension means you can explain it back.
I had a student whose comprehension doubled when she started asking herself one question after every page: what did I just read?
Simple trick. Check your comprehension as you go, and the long texts stop being scary.
And be patient with yourself. Comprehension grows slowly, like a plant. You water it with reading, and one day you notice it has grown.
Now, annotate. To annotate means to add short notes to a text as you read it. Underline a key sentence, write a question in the margin, star an important paragraph.
I love this one. When you annotate a chapter, you're having a conversation with the author. You write things like agree or example needed in the margin.
Teachers often ask students to annotate a poem with comments about the images and the mood. It slows you down in a good way.
And here's the secret: if you annotate carefully, you never have to reread the whole chapter. Your notes tell you exactly what mattered.
So grab a pen and annotate. Your future self, studying for the test, will thank you.

Halfway there. Our sixth word is retention. Retention is your ability to keep information in your memory over time.
This is the word scientists use. Good retention means you still remember the lesson next month, not just on test day.
And retention loves sleep. Studies show that your brain moves new words into long-term memory while you sleep, so retention improves when you rest well.
It also loves testing. Every time you quiz yourself, you strengthen retention of that material.
So protect your retention: sleep well, test yourself, and stop rereading the same page ten times.

Word seven is brainstorm. To brainstorm means to throw out lots of ideas quickly, usually in a group, without judging them yet.
Brainstorming is perfect for essays. Before you write, brainstorm ten possible angles and pick the best three.
I use it for everything. Stuck on a gift idea? Brainstorm with a friend. Stuck on a business name? Brainstorm twenty options in ten minutes.
The rule of a good brainstorm is simple: no criticism during the idea storm. You judge later.
Exactly. Brainstorm first, choose later. Quantity before quality, every time.
Our eighth word is summarize. To summarize means to give the main points of something in a short form.
After a long lecture, a good student can summarize it in three sentences. That's the skill right there.
Try it tonight: read a news article and summarize it for a friend in under a minute. If they get the idea, you did it well.
Summarizing forces your brain to decide what actually matters. It's one of the fastest ways to learn.
And teachers love it. When you summarize a chapter correctly, they know you understood it.
Word nine: paraphrase. To paraphrase means to say or write something again in your own words.
This is the honest way to use someone else's idea. Instead of copying a sentence, you paraphrase it and keep the meaning.
My writing teacher always said: if you can paraphrase a paragraph without looking at it, you truly understand it.
It's also a great test skill. Read a question, paraphrase it silently to yourself, and suddenly the answer is clearer.
So paraphrase often. It's respect for the original writer and a workout for your own brain.
