The Market Lesson

When I was twenty, I believed that a splurge was the best cure for a bad day.
Every Friday after work, I would splurge on clothes I barely wore.
My grandmother watched me splurge for a whole year without saying a word.
Then one Saturday she took me to the old market, where vendors peddle fruit, spices, and handmade soap.
She whispered that the ones who peddle with patience survive for decades.
She pointed at a quiet man who had peddled vegetables on the same corner for thirty years.
He told me he had learned to peddle honestly from his father.
"He never had to scrimp," my grandmother told me, "because he never learned to waste."
I laughed, because I had to scrimp every month just to pay my rent.
"You scrimp because you spend first and save later," she said, and she was right.
That day she taught me to scrimp with a purpose, not out of fear.
Across the street stood a new boutique, all glass and gold, painfully opulent.
Inside, everything was opulent, from the marble floor to the silk curtains.
The owner was famous for his opulent parties, and the pecuniary excess shocked even the wealthy guests.
But his spending was profligate, and every merchant in the market whispered about his debts.
He had a profligate habit of buying sports cars he never drove.
Within two years, his profligate empire collapsed and the opulent boutique stood empty.
On a rainy Tuesday, the court declared him insolvent.
Once a business is declared insolvent, suppliers stop calling and friends stop answering.
I decided that morning that I would never become insolvent, no matter how tempting the easy road looked.
My grandmother, by contrast, was famously parsimonious.
She was so parsimonious that she mended the same winter coat for fifteen years.
"Being parsimonious is not the same as being poor," she explained over tea.
She had quietly saved enough to help all three grandchildren, and she had never once needed a splurge to feel happy.
"Did you ever want to monetize the market?" I asked. "You know everyone here."
She smiled. "I could have monetized my recipes years ago, but some things are better left unpriced."
Years later, I finally monetized my own photography, and I understood exactly what she meant.
Her neighbor, a clever baker, had monetized her famous honey cake through a small online shop.
But the baker's son kept trying to upsell every customer at the counter.
"Would you like the large box? The gift wrapping? The premium honey?" His upsell never stopped.
My grandmother refused every upsell with a smile, and she taught me to do the same.
When the insolvent boutique owner went bankrupt after years of profligate spending, the lawyers argued about pecuniary damages for months.
The whole affair was a pecuniary disaster, with debts and fines piling up in every direction.
"Money is a tool," my grandmother said. "Its only pecuniary power is the freedom it buys."
Now I scrimp gladly, I question every upsell, and I save like my parsimonious grandmother taught me.
