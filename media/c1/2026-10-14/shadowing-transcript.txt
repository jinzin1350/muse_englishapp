Everyone said the Hendersons ran a monopoly on fresh food in the harbor district.
Their monopoly meant higher prices, and the fishermen's families quietly complained.
When a new co-op arrived, the old monopoly finally began to crack.
The city council investigated the monopoly and ordered the Hendersons to open their books.
Yet even after that, a loose oligopoly of three big wholesalers kept prices high.
The oligopoly fixed delivery fees, and small shopkeepers had no choice but to pay.
Some said the oligopoly was just smart business; others called it a trap.
Maya, a young procurement officer, joined the co-op that spring.
Her job in procurement was to find honest suppliers at fair prices.
The co-op's procurement team cut out the middlemen one by one.
She also learned that procurement can break an oligopoly faster than any law.
The old family firm could not compete, and rumors of liquidation spread fast.
Liquidation would mean auctions, empty shelves, and jobs lost overnight.
To avoid liquidation, the Hendersons hired a bank to restructure their debt.
The liquidation was postponed twice, but the debts kept growing.
Meanwhile, banks offered to underwrite a rescue loan for the firm.
To underwrite the loan, the bank demanded the family's warehouses as security.
The directors refused to underwrite anything without a guaranteed return.
Without someone to underwrite the rescue, the firm slid toward the edge.
A broker named Sami offered to sell the shares for a fair commission.
He asked only a small commission, paid when the deal was done.
The family promised him his commission in writing, witnessed by a lawyer.
A fair commission, Sami said, keeps everyone honest.
Across town, Lena received a remittance from her brother in Toronto, and that remittance arrived right on time.
His remittance arrived every month, regular as the tide.
The remittance paid for her mother's medicine and her son's school fees.
At the market, prices began a slow deflation that spring.
Farmers worried the deflation would shrink their earnings, even as shoppers cheered the deflation.
Still, gentle deflation meant ordinary families could finally afford more.
The co-op's final shipment was held in escrow until the invoice cleared.
Escrow protected the farmers when the old firm refused to pay on time, and escrow calmed every nervous supplier.
The lawyers called escrow the quiet guardian of every honest deal.
As an adviser, Maya held a fiduciary duty to the co-op's members.
A fiduciary duty, she explained, means putting others' interests first.
The co-op trusted her fiduciary judgment, and her fiduciary honesty helped the harbor slowly recover.
