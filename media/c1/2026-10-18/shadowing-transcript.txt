At forty, Daniel decided to become a true philomath.
A philomath, he told his friends, is someone who loves learning.
Every morning, the self-made philomath read one chapter before work.
His friends admired the philomath's curiosity and discipline.
He remembered his old scholastic days with mixed feelings.
His scholastic record was average, but his curiosity never died.
He wanted his adult studies to feel more meaningful than his scholastic years had.
He signed up for an evening course on classical literature.
The professor was warm and never didactic.
Some teachers are too didactic, Daniel thought, and their lessons die.
But this professor shared stories instead of didactic lectures.
He never gave pedantic corrections on small grammar slips.
A pedantic teacher would have marked every tiny mistake.
Daniel was grateful his teacher was not pedantic.
Each week, students wrote a short exegesis of a poem.
Daniel had never written an exegesis before.
His first exegesis was clumsy, but his teacher praised the effort.
By the third month, his exegesis showed real insight.
Writing an exegesis taught him to read slowly and think deeply.
The daily routine began to inculcate strong study habits in him.
His teacher's gentle repetition helped inculcate the new vocabulary.
Slowly, the course began to inculcate confidence too.
He dreamed of formal study and planned to matriculate in autumn.
To matriculate at a real university had always been his dream.
His friends cheered when he finally decided to matriculate, and the applause seemed to inculcate courage in him.
After you matriculate, his advisor said, the real work begins.
He looked forward to scholastic life as an adult student.
His scholastic ambitions now felt real and reachable.
Being a philomath had changed how he saw himself.
He was no longer afraid of didactic books or pedantic rules, because now he understood them.
What makes a philomath different, he realized, is joy, not talent.
Once you matriculate, nothing can inculcate discipline better than a deadline.
He smiled at his didactic bookshelf, where even the most pedantic grammar guides now looked friendly.
