Maya: To saunter is to walk slowly, calmly, and confidently, as if you own the street and the afternoon. When you saunter, you are in no hurry at all. Picture someone who can saunter along a promenade at sunset, hands in pockets, smiling at nothing in particular.
Lena: The writer Thoreau once said that to saunter is almost a kind of holy walking, like a pilgrim with nowhere urgent to be. I try to saunter through airports, which is ridiculous, because everyone else is sprinting.

Maya: To circumnavigate means to travel all the way around something, usually the earth. Magellan's expedition was the first to circumnavigate the planet, though Magellan himself did not survive the voyage.
Lena: Right, and today a small but fearless group of solo sailors still sets out to circumnavigate the world alone. Imagine that. Months at sea, just you and the horizon, trying to circumnavigate an entire planet in a boat shorter than a bus.

Lena: What I find moving is that every person who sets out to circumnavigate the earth is repeating a dream that is five hundred years old. The technology changes, but the urge to circumnavigate, to draw a full circle around the world, never fades.

Lena: A waystation is a stopping place on a long journey, somewhere travelers rest, eat, and exchange news before moving on. In ancient times, the caravanserai was the classic waystation, a fortified inn on the Silk Road where camels, merchants, and stories all rested together.
Maya: I love the idea that every waystation is also a meeting place. Think of a tiny mountain café that serves as a waystation for hikers, or even a big airport transit hotel, which is really just a modern waystation with better lighting.
Lena: Exactly. And some towns exist almost entirely as a waystation. They grew up because travelers needed them, and they keep their character from centuries of strangers passing through. Without the waystation, the long journey would be unbearable.
Maya: Every journey needs its pauses. And every journey needs its ending, which brings us to terminus. What is a terminus, Lena?
Lena: A terminus is the station at the very end of a railway line, where the tracks simply stop. Think of the great European terminus stations with their vast arched roofs, places where thousands of journeys end every single day.
Maya: There is something emotional about a terminus. You step off the train at the terminus, and the journey is officially over. No more motion, no more anticipation. The terminus forces you to arrive, really arrive.
Lena: And writers love the word as a metaphor, too. They will call something the terminus of an era, or the terminus of a dream. It sounds so much more final, more architectural, than just saying the end.
Maya: It does. Though I will admit, I have mixed feelings at every terminus. Relief to arrive, sadness that the moving part is done. A good terminus should probably have a café, so you can sit with that feeling for a while.
Lena: Now let us rewind to the very beginning of a voyage. Our eighth word is embarkation. Maya, why is embarkation such a romantic word?
Maya: Embarkation is the formal act of boarding a ship or an aircraft at the start of a journey. There is a ceremony to embarkation, the gangway, the announcements, the moment the doors close. Embarkation is the threshold between ordinary life and adventure.
Lena: That is exactly it. The hour before embarkation is electric. You have said your goodbyes, your bag is packed, and embarkation turns waiting into leaving. I always arrive early just to savor the embarkation atmosphere in the terminal.
Maya: And historically, embarkation was a huge public event. Whole towns would gather at the docks to watch an embarkation, waving handkerchiefs as the ship pulled away. We have lost some of that pageantry, but the word embarkation keeps the romance alive.
Lena: It really does. Our ninth word takes us to the edges of the map. Far-flung. Maya, what makes a place far-flung?
Maya: A far-flung place is extremely remote, distant from the centers of the world. Think of far-flung islands in the Pacific, or a far-flung research station in Antarctica. The phrase far-flung always carries a little thrill of inaccessibility.
Lena: I have a friend who collects postcards from far-flung outposts, the more far-flung the better. She says a letter from a far-flung village feels like a message from another century.
Maya: And in our connected age, the far-flung still exists, which is kind of miraculous. You can video-call someone in a far-flung corner of the planet, and yet that place remains far-flung in spirit, wild, distant, and a little mysterious.
Lena: Beautiful. Our final word is for the people who draw the maps that guide all these journeys. Cartography. Lena, tell us about it.
Maya: Cartography is the art and science of making maps. For centuries, cartography was one of the most powerful skills in the world, because whoever controlled cartography controlled how people imagined the earth.
Lena: The old masters of cartography were artists as much as scientists. Their cartography filled unknown oceans with sea monsters and golden cities. Every piece of antique cartography is a portrait of what its age believed and feared.
Maya: And modern cartography is no less fascinating. Satellite cartography maps the planet down to the centimeter, yet digital cartography still makes choices, what to label, what to hide, what to emphasize. All cartography is an argument about what matters.
