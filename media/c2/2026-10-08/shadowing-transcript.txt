The Reunion

This year, the Hartley family reunion at the old lake house carried a quiet hope: a rapprochement between Daniel and his younger brother Marcus.
The two had not spoken in six years; their estrangement had started with a careless remark at a wedding, but pride had turned it into years of silence.
Their mother believed that one honest conversation could begin a true rapprochement.
But rapprochement cannot be rushed, and everyone knew the first evening would be tense.
Nobody wanted the estrangement to become permanent, so this reunion felt like a last chance.
Elena had watched the estrangement deepen with quiet sadness every single year.
Daniel arrived with his wife Elena, to whom he was famously uxorious.
He cancelled his golf trips for her and planned every holiday around her, and his friends teased him for being so uxorious.
Being uxorious suited Daniel; devotion simply came naturally to him.
Elena only laughed, because an uxorious husband who cooks dinner every night is nothing to complain about.
Marcus arrived alone, still carrying the unrequited feelings he had held for a college friend for a decade, having finally accepted that his unrequited love would never be returned.
There is a special sadness to unrequited devotion, his sister told him, but it teaches you what you truly value.
He kept her old letters in a drawer, a quiet monument to unrequited hope.
Their uncle Robert greeted everyone at the door with his usual avuncular warmth.
Robert had the most avuncular laugh, the kind that made nervous newcomers feel instantly welcome.
Children adored him, and his avuncular advice had guided three generations through trouble.
Even Marcus, who trusted almost no one, softened under his uncle's avuncular kindness.
At dinner, the cousins recalled the fierce camaraderie of their childhood summers, when building rafts together had created a camaraderie that adulthood never quite broke.
Someone raised a glass to old times, and the room filled with the easy camaraderie of people who truly know one another.
It was the kind of camaraderie that makes a family feel unbreakable, at least for one evening.
After dinner, Daniel's teenage daughter confessed her limerence for a boy from her literature class, and her mother smiled, remembering her own youthful limerence.
Limerence fades, her father said gently, but the tenderness it leaves behind can grow into something real.
She rolled her eyes, convinced that no one had ever felt limerence as powerfully as she did.
Later, by the lake, old Thomas told the story of his secret tryst with his late wife, a midnight tryst arranged in this very garden long before their families approved.
That tryst became family legend, proof that love finds a way even when the world objects.
Nobody judged the old tryst anymore; it had become the family's favorite romance.
By midnight, over coffee on the porch, the two brothers finally began their fragile rapprochement.
It was a small step, but it ended the long estrangement that had shadowed every holiday for years.
The conversation then turned to Adrian, the scion of the wealthy family across the lake, who as the scion of a shipping empire had been expected to take over the company at twenty-five.
Instead, the young scion had become a marine biologist, and the lake house crowd admired his courage.
His grandmother still introduced him as the scion of the family, half proud and half bewildered.
Talk of Adrian reminded everyone how fickle fortune and public opinion can be, loving you in spring and forgetting you by autumn.
Even friendships can be fickle when distance and busy lives get in the way.
A fickle heart rarely means to cause harm, Robert added, but the damage is real all the same.
That is why, he added, we must guard against our own fickle attention and show up for the people we love.
