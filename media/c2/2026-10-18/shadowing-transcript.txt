The River Remembers

Every spring, my grandmother's biophilia pulled her back to the same wild riverbank.
She said the anthropocene could not touch the place where she grew up.
Back then, nobody spoke of bioremediation, because the river ran clean.
The old willows along the bank were riparian giants, older than any living soul.
In those days, the garden woke each year with vernal energy that smelled of rain.
My grandmother believed that biophilia was not a luxury but a human need.
She taught us that the anthropocene began long before we named it.
When the factory upstream started leaking oil, the village feared the worst.
Scientists arrived with a gentle plan called bioremediation.
They planted reeds and let their roots begin the slow work of bioremediation.
Year after year, the reeds filtered the poisons from the mud.
Slowly, the river entered a new stage of succession, from sludge to marsh to meadow.
Nature's succession moved quietly, reeds first, then grasses, then young willows.
The otters returned first, sliding down the muddy banks at dawn.
Then the kingfishers came, bright flashes over the riparian water.
My uncle, who had lost all hope, felt his old biophilia waking again.
He said the anthropocene was not the end of the story, only a hard chapter.
Beyond the river, the hills had suffered a different wound: defaunation.
Poachers had emptied the hills through years of silent defaunation.
The forest stood green but hollow, the classic signature of defaunation.
The village voted to guard the hills, and to fight defaunation with patrols and laws.
Within five years, deer tracks appeared in the vernal mud of the lower trails.
Wolves followed, and the whole valley breathed in a deep, vernal sigh of relief.
We watched succession climb the hillsides too, shrubs rising where goats had grazed bare earth.
Each stage of succession brought new birds, new insects, new life.
The children now learn the names of riparian plants on school walks along the river.
They draw maps of the riparian zone and count the willows one by one.
My grandmother's biophilia lives on in every child who wades into that clean water.
The story of our river proves that even deep in the anthropocene, repair is possible.
Bioremediation, patience, and love: that is all succession asks of us.
And when the vernal rain falls on riparian leaves, I hear her laughing again.
Defaunation taught us what loss feels like; biophilia taught us how to come home.
