Karim was a clever merchant in the old bazaar of Tabriz.
Every morning he watched two markets, and he loved arbitrage.
He practiced arbitrage by comparing prices between the morning bazaar and the evening square.
His friends laughed, but his arbitrage earned him a fortune.
Soon everyone copied his arbitrage, and the prices became equal.
When arbitrage died, Karim learned a painful lesson about liquidity.
He had bought carpets with terrible liquidity, and he could not sell them.
Cash has perfect liquidity, but carpets do not.
To protect his liquidity, he started to hedge every big purchase.
He would hedge by keeping half his money in gold.
His gold was his hedge against bad seasons.
One spring, a windfall changed everything.
A rich traveler paid double for his carpets, a true windfall.
This windfall gave him back the liquidity he had lost.
With his windfall secure, he examined the solvency of every partner.
He refused to trade with merchants of doubtful solvency, keeping his pecuniary standards strict.
Solvency, he said, matters more than charm.
His decisions were never emotional; they were purely pecuniary.
A pecuniary mind sees numbers, not flattery.
He avoided usurious lenders who offered quick cash at cruel rates.
Their usurious contracts had ruined his father, a victim of usurious greed.
Karim was famously parsimonious with his own spending.
His parsimonious habits annoyed his wife but saved his business.
Even in success he stayed parsimonious, eating simple bread and cheese.
He never trusted speculation, for speculation had destroyed greater men.
The speculation of others was his warning, not his game.
When the great bank of the city needed a bailout, Karim smiled quietly.
The government bailout rescued the reckless, not the careful.
He had needed no bailout because his hedge and his solvency had protected him.
Years later, young traders asked him for the secret of his wealth.
He answered: master arbitrage, respect liquidity, always hedge, welcome every windfall, guard your solvency, think in pecuniary terms, shun usurious debt, live parsimonious, avoid speculation, and never beg for a bailout.
