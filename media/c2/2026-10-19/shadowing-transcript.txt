Every March, a small institute by the river announced its vernal research season with quiet pride.
This year the director, Dr. Lena Marsh, proposed something bold: a year-long experiment in bioremediation.
The river was choked with industrial runoff, and she argued that only bioremediation could clean it without poison.
Her team would plant reeds and seed microbes along the banks, letting bioremediation do the slow, patient work.
The project was funded by a technocratic council that controlled the city's entire environmental budget.
The council's decisions were always technocratic; no public debate, just experts signing off in a windowless room.
Some citizens called the arrangement technocratic overreach, but the funding was already secured.
Their proposal rested on a strange new philosophy sweeping the city's think tanks: accelerationism.
Accelerationism claimed that progress should be sped up, not restrained by caution or red tape.
Its advocates preached accelerationism from podcast studios while the rest of the city argued about potholes.
The institute's young data team embraced technosolutionism with the enthusiasm of true believers.
To them, technosolutionism meant every social problem could be solved by building the right algorithm.
The older engineers warned that pure technosolutionism would crash against reality sooner or later.
Cybernetics had taught Dr. Marsh that machines and living things speak the same language of feedback.
She applied cybernetics to the river project, designing sensors that learned from the reeds themselves.
The real lesson of cybernetics, she said, was humility before systems too complex to fully control.
For quick daily decisions she relied on a simple heuristic: if it harms the river, don't do it.
The heuristic was crude, but it beat a fifty-page manual that nobody ever opened.
Every morning the team repeated the heuristic like a mantra before touching a single instrument.
Then came the scandal: the institute's donor portal was exposed as using a dark pattern.
The cancel button was hidden in pale grey text, a dark pattern designed to trap subscribers.
Investigators found the dark pattern had quietly siphoned money from thousands of donors for years.
That autumn belonged to the Anthropocene, or so the newspapers claimed in their autumn specials.
Scientists said the Anthropocene was written into the river mud itself, in layers of plastic and lead.
Whether we name it the Anthropocene or not, they argued, humans have become a geological force.
In the evenings, the youngest researcher, Farah, returned to her notes for quiet lucubration.
Her lucubration took her past midnight, reading papers no one else in the lab had opened.
It was during one such lucubration that she found the key to the whole mystery.
The vernal reeds, she realized, were failing because the sensors were calibrated for a colder spring.
With vernal corrections applied, the wetland bloomed brighter than anyone had dared to hope.
