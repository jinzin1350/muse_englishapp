The Career Crossroads

Sara stared at her office ceiling and wondered when her job had stopped making sense.
She had golden handcuffs in the form of a bonus she would lose if she resigned.
Every morning she arrived exhausted, a classic case of presenteeism.
Her manager praised her attendance, but presenteeism was quietly destroying her health.
She decided to fight her presenteeism by taking real sick days without guilt.
Slowly, without telling anyone, she began quiet quitting.
Her quiet quitting meant no more late nights and no more weekend emails.
At first her quiet quitting felt like freedom, then it felt like emptiness.
She realized quiet quitting was a signal, not a solution.
She remembered her old dream of becoming an intrapreneur inside a bigger company.
An intrapreneur, she thought, could change things without starting from zero.
She pitched a small idea to her director, testing her intrapreneur instincts.
The pitch worked, and her intrapreneur spirit finally woke up.
To build credibility, she started writing about her industry online.
Her articles slowly built genuine thought leadership among her peers.
With growing thought leadership, invitations to speak began to arrive.
She learned that thought leadership opens doors a resume never could.
On weekends she began moonlighting as a freelance consultant.
Her moonlighting income paid for a design course she had always wanted.
Moonlighting taught her more in six months than her job had in three years.
She dreamed of turning that moonlighting into a full portfolio career.
A portfolio career would let her mix consulting, teaching, and writing.
She mapped out her portfolio career on paper and felt excited again.
Building a portfolio career takes patience, she told herself, one client at a time.
The boldest step would be a complete career pivot into product design.
A career pivot at forty felt terrifying and thrilling at once.
She planned her career pivot carefully, saving money for one full year.
Every career pivot needs a first small step, so she grabbed the low-hanging fruit and redesigned a friend's website.
That night, impostor syndrome whispered that she was not a real designer, and that impostor syndrome grew louder with every doubt.
She named her impostor syndrome out loud, and that impostor syndrome lost its power.
Her golden handcuffs finally released her when the three-year bonus was paid.
She left without anger, grateful that the golden handcuffs had funded her savings.
She promised herself never to confuse golden handcuffs with real happiness again.
With clear eyes, she saw that presenteeism had stolen two years of her energy, but the low-hanging fruit of small wins kept her moving.
She kept publishing, and her thought leadership grew, while the low-hanging fruit of her old network brought her first clients, and she celebrated every piece of low-hanging fruit.
