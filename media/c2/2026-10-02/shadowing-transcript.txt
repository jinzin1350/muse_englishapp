A City of Bright Shadows

Dara moved to a smart city where even the buses knew his name.
On his first day, the city's app greeted him, and the disintermediation of old bureaucracy felt like freedom.
No more offices, no more queues: the disintermediation had reached his doctor, his landlord, even his voting booth.
But his phone was already showing signs of obsolescence after only two years.
The repair shop blamed planned obsolescence, and the obsolescence felt personal.
Cameras watched him from every corner, a panoptic net over the whole city.
In a panoptic world, he whispered, privacy becomes a rumor.
The panoptic glow of the streetlights followed him home at night.
His banking app still used a skeuomorph, a fake leather wallet on a glass screen.
The skeuomorph made his grandmother smile; the skeuomorph made him laugh.
Then his loan application was rejected by a black box he could not question.
The bank's black box gave no reasons, only a red light and a cold number.
Nobody at the bank could open the black box, not even the engineers.
A chatbot told him a fake law that had never existed; a perfect hallucination.
The hallucination was confident, smooth, and completely invented.
He almost believed the hallucination because it sounded so certain.
That evening, a video of the mayor confessing appeared online: a deepfake.
The deepfake spread faster than any truth, and no deepfake ever waits for proof.
His neighbor, a cheerful neoluddite, had warned him about days like this.
The neoluddite lived without apps, and the neoluddite slept well at night.
Yet even the neoluddite admitted that some progress was worth keeping.
Some scientists at the university talked about the singularity, the day machines outthink us.
They said the singularity might arrive quietly, not with thunder but with updates.
Others laughed at the singularity and called it a bedtime story for engineers.
Meanwhile the datafication of his life continued: steps, sleep, heartbeats, moods.
Every run he took fed the datafication machine another thousand data points.
The datafication of friendship hurt most of all: his friends had become scores.
He realized datafication had turned his body into a spreadsheet.
The disintermediation of the banks had only built bigger banks, and the disintermediation of his newsfeed had built a smaller world.
What good is fighting obsolescence, he asked, if the panoptic city owns your reflection?
So Dara started writing on paper, a small rebellion, half neoluddite and half dreamer.
On paper, no hallucination could trick him, and no deepfake could wear his face.
He kept one app though, with its silly skeuomorph, because comfort matters too.
The black box stayed a mystery, but the singularity, he decided, could wait.
Progress, he learned, is neither angel nor monster; it is a tool, and tools need hands.
