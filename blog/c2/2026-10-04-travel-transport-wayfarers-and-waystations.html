📚 Vocabulary

10 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
peregrine /PEH-ruh-grin/
adjective
traveling or wandering far from home
"After university, she spent a peregrine year drifting through South America with only a backpack."
itinerant /ih-TIN-uh-ruhnt/
adjective
traveling from place to place for work
"The itinerant photographer sold prints in every town along the coast."
meander /mee-AN-der/
verb
to wander slowly without a fixed direction
"We meandered through the old quarter, stopping whenever a doorway caught our eye."
saunter /SAWN-ter/
verb
to walk slowly and confidently, in no hurry
"He sauntered down to the harbour as if he had all the time in the world."
circumnavigate /sir-kum-NAV-ih-gayt/
verb
to travel all the way around the earth
"It took her eleven months to circumnavigate the globe by sail alone."
waystation /WAY-stay-shun/
noun
a stopping place on a long journey
"The tiny desert town had been a waystation for traders crossing the dunes for centuries."
terminus /TUR-mih-nus/
noun
the final station at the end of a railway line
"We finally reached the terminus at midnight, exhausted but exhilarated."
embarkation /em-bar-KAY-shun/
noun
the act of boarding a ship or aircraft
"Embarkation begins two hours before departure, so allow plenty of time."
far-flung /far-FLUNG/
adjective
extremely remote and distant
"He collects stamps from far-flung islands most people could not find on a map."
cartography /kar-TOG-ruh-fee/
noun
the art and science of making maps
"Early cartography was as much art as science, filling blank oceans with sea monsters."

Podcast: Travel & Transport: Wayfarers and Waystations

Listen to today’s conversation.

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Hello and welcome back to Word Kitchen, the show where we cook up the English language one delicious word at a time. I am Maya.

Lena

And I am Lena. Today we are going on a journey, and I mean that almost literally. Our theme is the art of travel, told through ten beautiful, sophisticated words.

Maya

That is right. We have words for wanderers, for slow walkers, for mapmakers, and for the far corners of the earth. So pack a bag, and let us begin with a word that sounds like it belongs in a poem. Lena, what is a peregrine traveler?

Lena

A peregrine traveler is someone who wanders far from home, guided more by curiosity than by a plan. The word peregrine comes from the Latin for someone journeying abroad, and it carries this lovely sense of roaming the world with open eyes.

Maya

I love that. I once met a peregrine soul in a night train crossing the Balkans. She had no fixed destination. She told me she had been living a peregrine life for three years, moving from city to city, working wherever she landed.

Lena

That is the dream for so many people, is it not? To be a little bit peregrine. Not lost, exactly, but deliberately unrooted. There is even the peregrine falcon, the bird that migrates enormous distances every year. So the word carries both freedom and endurance.

Maya

Beautifully put. And it makes me think of the difference between a tourist and a peregrine wanderer. A tourist follows a schedule. A peregrine traveler follows a feeling.

Lena

Speaking of people who travel for a living, that brings us to our second word, which is itinerant. Maya, how would you describe an itinerant life?

Maya

An itinerant worker is someone who moves from place to place for work. Think of an itinerant musician playing in a different town every weekend, or an itinerant nurse taking short contracts in rural hospitals.

Lena

Exactly. The classic image is the itinerant craftsman of centuries past, going door to door with his tools. But the modern version is everywhere. The freelance photographer with an itinerant schedule, shooting weddings on three continents in one summer.

Maya

And honestly, so many digital nomads today are living an itinerant life without using the word. They rent a desk in Lisbon for two months, then Bangkok for two months. It is the old itinerant pattern with faster Wi-Fi.

Lena

Ha, that is true. Though I think the word itinerant keeps a slightly romantic, old-world flavor. It suggests someone with a trade, a skill they carry with them, not just a laptop and a beach.

Maya

Agreed. Now, whether you are peregrine or itinerant, the best travelers share a habit, and our next word names it. Lena, do you ever just meander?

Lena

Oh, constantly. To meander is to wander without a fixed direction, the way a river meanders through a valley. When I arrive in a new city, my first day is always a meander. No map, no list, just walking.

Maya

That is the perfect first day. I remember landing in Marrakech years ago and deciding to simply meander through the medina. Every alley seemed to meander into another alley, and I got gloriously lost for hours.

Lena

And that is the thing. A journey that never meanders is just transportation. The meander is where the discoveries happen. Even rivers know this. A river that does not meander is a canal, efficient but dull.

Maya

I love that image. So our advice to listeners: build a meander into every trip. Let your feet meander, let your eyes meander, and the city will show you things no guidebook can.

Lena

Our next word is a close cousin of meandering, but with more style. Maya, what does it mean to saunter?

Maya

To saunter is to walk slowly, calmly, and confidently, as if you own the street and the afternoon. When you saunter, you are in no hurry at all. Picture someone who can saunter along a promenade at sunset, hands in pockets, smiling at nothing in particular.

Lena

The writer Thoreau once said that to saunter is almost a kind of holy walking, like a pilgrim with nowhere urgent to be. I try to saunter through airports, which is ridiculous, because everyone else is sprinting.

Maya

That is the ultimate test. Can you saunter when the departure board is flashing? The saunter is a small rebellion against hurry. Next time you are in a beautiful place, do not march through it. Saunter through it.

Lena

And let the hurried crowds stream past you while you saunter at your own royal pace. Wonderful. Now let us go bigger, much bigger. Our fifth word is for the most ambitious journeys of all. Maya, what does it mean to circumnavigate the globe?

Maya

To circumnavigate means to travel all the way around something, usually the earth. Magellan's expedition was the first to circumnavigate the planet, though Magellan himself did not survive the voyage.

Lena

Right, and today a small but fearless group of solo sailors still sets out to circumnavigate the world alone. Imagine that. Months at sea, just you and the horizon, trying to circumnavigate an entire planet in a boat shorter than a bus.

Maya

It is the ultimate test of seamanship. The first woman to circumnavigate the globe solo did it in the late nineteen sixties, and she described the loneliness as the hardest part, harder than the storms.

Lena

What I find moving is that every person who sets out to circumnavigate the earth is repeating a dream that is five hundred years old. The technology changes, but the urge to circumnavigate, to draw a full circle around the world, never fades.

Maya

From the grandest voyages, let us come back down to the small, human pauses along the way. Our sixth word is waystation. Lena, paint me a picture.

Lena

A waystation is a stopping place on a long journey, somewhere travelers rest, eat, and exchange news before moving on. In ancient times, the caravanserai was the classic waystation, a fortified inn on the Silk Road where camels, merchants, and stories all rested together.

Maya

I love the idea that every waystation is also a meeting place. Think of a tiny mountain café that serves as a waystation for hikers, or even a big airport transit hotel, which is really just a modern waystation with better lighting.

Lena

Exactly. And some towns exist almost entirely as a waystation. They grew up because travelers needed them, and they keep their character from centuries of strangers passing through. Without the waystation, the long journey would be unbearable.

Maya

Every journey needs its pauses. And every journey needs its ending, which brings us to terminus. What is a terminus, Lena?

Lena

A terminus is the station at the very end of a railway line, where the tracks simply stop. Think of the great European terminus stations with their vast arched roofs, places where thousands of journeys end every single day.

Maya

There is something emotional about a terminus. You step off the train at the terminus, and the journey is officially over. No more motion, no more anticipation. The terminus forces you to arrive, really arrive.

Lena

And writers love the word as a metaphor, too. They will call something the terminus of an era, or the terminus of a dream. It sounds so much more final, more architectural, than just saying the end.

Maya

It does. Though I will admit, I have mixed feelings at every terminus. Relief to arrive, sadness that the moving part is done. A good terminus should probably have a café, so you can sit with that feeling for a while.

Lena

Now let us rewind to the very beginning of a voyage. Our eighth word is embarkation. Maya, why is embarkation such a romantic word?

Maya

Embarkation is the formal act of boarding a ship or an aircraft at the start of a journey. There is a ceremony to embarkation, the gangway, the announcements, the moment the doors close. Embarkation is the threshold between ordinary life and adventure.

Lena

That is exactly it. The hour before embarkation is electric. You have said your goodbyes, your bag is packed, and embarkation turns waiting into leaving. I always arrive early just to savor the embarkation atmosphere in the terminal.

Maya

And historically, embarkation was a huge public event. Whole towns would gather at the docks to watch an embarkation, waving handkerchiefs as the ship pulled away. We have lost some of that pageantry, but the word embarkation keeps the romance alive.

Lena

It really does. Our ninth word takes us to the edges of the map. Far-flung. Maya, what makes a place far-flung?

Maya

A far-flung place is extremely remote, distant from the centers of the world. Think of far-flung islands in the Pacific, or a far-flung research station in Antarctica. The phrase far-flung always carries a little thrill of inaccessibility.

Lena

I have a friend who collects postcards from far-flung outposts, the more far-flung the better. She says a letter from a far-flung village feels like a message from another century.

Maya

And in our connected age, the far-flung still exists, which is kind of miraculous. You can video-call someone in a far-flung corner of the planet, and yet that place remains far-flung in spirit, wild, distant, and a little mysterious.

Lena

Beautiful. Our final word is for the people who draw the maps that guide all these journeys. Cartography. Lena, tell us about it.

Maya

Cartography is the art and science of making maps. For centuries, cartography was one of the most powerful skills in the world, because whoever controlled cartography controlled how people imagined the earth.

Lena

The old masters of cartography were artists as much as scientists. Their cartography filled unknown oceans with sea monsters and golden cities. Every piece of antique cartography is a portrait of what its age believed and feared.

Maya

And modern cartography is no less fascinating. Satellite cartography maps the planet down to the centimeter, yet digital cartography still makes choices, what to label, what to hide, what to emphasize. All cartography is an argument about what matters.

Lena

That is a profound thought to end on. From the peregrine wanderer to the science of cartography, every journey needs both the dreamer and the mapmaker.

Maya

Let us recap our ten words for the road. A peregrine traveler roams far from home. An itinerant worker travels from place to place for work. To meander is to wander without direction. To saunter is to walk slowly and confidently. To circumnavigate is to travel all the way around the earth. A waystation is a resting place on a long journey. A terminus is the final station where the line ends. Embarkation is the act of boarding at the start of a voyage. Far-flung means extremely remote. And cartography is the art and science of mapmaking.

Lena

Ten words, one grand journey. Try using one of them this week. Tell a friend you are going to meander through the old town, or saunter to the café instead of rushing. Language is a journey too.

Maya

Thanks for traveling with us on Word Kitchen. I am Maya.

Lena

And I am Lena. Until next time, keep wandering.

📝Grammar: Past and present habits and states — used to, would, and the past simple

**used to** + infinitive describes a past habit or state that is finished: I used to teach English. It has no present form — never say *I use to*. The negative and question drop the d: **didn't use to**, **did you use to**. In conversation, **never used to** often replaces **didn't use to**. For repeated past actions, **would** + infinitive adds a nostalgic, storytelling tone: every summer we would sail to the islands.
