📚 Vocabulary

7 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
conceited /kun-SEE-tid/
adjective
Excessively proud of oneself; arrogant.
"His conceited attitude makes him difficult to work with."
conciliate /kun-SIL-ee-ayt/
verb
To stop someone from being angry; to make peace.
"He tried to conciliate the angry customers with a refund."
conscience /KON-shuns/
noun
An inner feeling guiding right or wrong behavior.
"His conscience bothered him after he lied."
covet /KUV-it/
verb
To desire something belonging to another.
"She coveted her neighbor's beautiful new car."
crave /KRAYV/
verb
To have a very strong desire for something.
"After a long day, I always crave a hot cup of tea."
cross your legs /KROS yor LEGZ/
verb phrase
To place one leg over the other while sitting.
"She sat elegantly, crossing her legs during the interview."
defuse /dee-FYOOZ/
verb
To make a dangerous or difficult situation calmer.
"He tried to defuse the tension with a joke."

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome to Muse English! Hello everyone, and welcome back to another episode. I am Maya, and as always, I am here with my wonderful co-host Lena.

Lena

Hi everyone! Great to be here again, Maya. And today we have a really interesting topic, because we are talking about character and emotions, the words we use to describe people, how they feel, and how they behave.

Maya

Exactly. Today we are looking at seven powerful words: conceited, conciliate, conscience, covet, crave, cross your legs, and defuse. By the end of this episode, you will be able to use every one of them confidently, in real conversations.

Lena

Let us start with conceited. A conceited person is excessively proud of themselves, arrogant in a way that puts other people off. Maya, have you ever worked with someone conceited?

Maya

Oh yes. I once worked with a man who was so conceited that he could not listen to anyone else in the room. Every meeting was about his ideas, his success, his talent. A conceited attitude like that makes teamwork almost impossible, because nobody wants to work with someone who thinks they are always right.

Lena

That is the key difference. Confidence is attractive, but a conceited person crosses the line. A confident person says, I did good work. A conceited person says, nobody here is as good as me. The conceited version always sounds louder, and emptier.

Maya

And here is the funny thing. Truly talented people are rarely conceited. The most conceited people I have met were often the least secure. Their conceited words were a kind of armour, hiding the fact that they were afraid of being judged.

Lena

That is so true. So if you meet someone conceited, remember that confidence and arrogance are not the same thing at all.

Maya

Before we go deeper, a quick word about something that makes all of this possible.

Lena

If you love learning English this way, you should try the Muse English app. It is completely free, and every day it gives you new words with photos and pronunciation, plus this podcast, shadowing stories, fun quizzes, grammar lessons, and a leaderboard where you can collect points. It takes less than a minute to install. Download it today and keep your English growing, one day at a time.

Maya

Now, back to our topic. Let us talk about two words that belong together: conciliate and defuse. Imagine a tense meeting. Two colleagues are arguing, voices are rising, and someone has to step in.

Lena

That someone needs to conciliate. To conciliate means to stop someone from being angry, to make peace. A good manager will conciliate the angry customer, or conciliate two colleagues who disagree, by listening first and finding common ground.

Maya

Exactly. And the best tool for that is to defuse the tension. To defuse means to make a dangerous or difficult situation calmer. A joke can defuse a tense room. A calm voice can defuse an argument before it explodes.

Lena

I love that word, defuse, because it comes from the world of bombs, taking the fuse out so nothing explodes. When you defuse a situation, you remove the danger. You defuse the tension with humour, or you defuse a conflict by giving everyone time to breathe.

Maya

And here is a useful tip. To conciliate someone, start by agreeing with one small thing they said. That one small agreement can defuse the whole argument. People stop fighting when they feel heard.

Lena

So remember the pair. Conciliate is the goal, making peace. Defuse is the method, calming things down. A wise person knows how to conciliate an angry friend and how to defuse a difficult conversation.

Maya

Let us move to a quieter word now, conscience. Your conscience is the inner feeling that guides you between right and wrong. When you do something wrong, your conscience bothers you. It is that small voice inside that says, that was not right.

Lena

That is a beautiful word. A clear conscience lets you sleep well at night. A guilty conscience keeps you awake. Maya, do you think conscience is something we are born with, or something we learn?

Maya

I think it is a little of both. Children learn from their families, but the feeling itself is universal. When his conscience bothered him after he lied, the man in our example knew he had to apologise. His conscience was telling him the truth, even when his pride was telling him to stay silent.

Lena

And that is where conscience meets our first word. A conceited person often ignores their conscience, because pride is louder than that quiet inner voice. But sooner or later, the conscience speaks, and a wise person listens.

Maya

That is a lovely connection. So conscience is not just a feeling. It is a guide. Listen to your conscience, and it will keep your character honest.

Lena

We are about halfway through, so here is another friendly reminder.

Maya

If you are enjoying this episode, open the Muse English app and explore even more. You get daily words with clear photos and pronunciation audio, this podcast, shadowing stories you can practise with, quizzes that test what you learned, grammar lessons that make the rules simple, and points and a leaderboard to keep you motivated. It is free, it is fun, and it is waiting for you.

Lena

Now, Maya, let us talk about desire, because two of our words today are all about wanting things: covet and crave.

Maya

Yes. To covet means to desire something that belongs to another person. She coveted her neighbour's beautiful new car. She did not just admire it. She wanted it for herself, and that wanting made her unhappy.

Lena

Covet is a strong word, and it often has a negative feeling. When you covet what others have, you stop enjoying what you already own. People covet their neighbour's house, their colleague's promotion, or a stranger's perfect life on social media.

Maya

And that is the danger of coveting. The more you covet, the less satisfied you feel. A conceited person covets attention, a jealous person covets success, and both of them miss the good things right in front of them.

Lena

Crave is different. To crave means to have a very strong desire for something, but it is not always about other people. After a long day, I always crave a hot cup of tea. That is a simple, honest craving.

Maya

Exactly. You can crave food, rest, quiet, or a walk in fresh air. Athletes crave water after training. Parents crave sleep. We crave the things our body and mind need.

Lena

But be careful, because crave can also be strong. Some people crave sugar, some crave attention, and some crave success so much that it controls them. The difference is simple. Covet is wanting what belongs to someone else. Crave is a deep desire, for anything at all.

Maya

That is a perfect summary. So ask yourself: do I covet my neighbour's life, or do I simply crave a little more peace? One makes you bitter. The other just makes you human.

Lena

Now our last word is a phrase, and it is about body language: cross your legs. To cross your legs means to place one leg over the other while sitting. She sat elegantly, crossing her legs during the interview.

Maya

Body language matters so much. When you cross your legs in a job interview, you can look calm and confident. But in some cultures, to cross your legs in front of an older person is considered rude, so it is worth knowing the local customs.

Lena

That is a great point. In some places, people rarely cross their legs in formal meetings, while in others, everyone does it without thinking.

Maya

And here is a fun detail. When someone is relaxed, they often cross their legs without noticing. When someone is nervous, they might cross their legs tightly, almost like a shield. Your legs tell a story, even when your mouth is silent.

Lena

So our phrase is simple, but it opens a whole conversation about culture and body language. Next time you sit down, notice: do you cross your legs, or do you sit with both feet on the floor?

Maya

Let us put all seven words together with a little story. Imagine Sara. Sara works in an office with a conceited manager who loves to hear his own voice.

Lena

One Monday, the manager's conceited comments made the whole team angry, and Sara had to conciliate the situation before the meeting fell apart. She used a joke to defuse the tension, and suddenly everyone could breathe again.

Maya

That evening, Sara's conscience bothered her, because she had stayed silent when the manager was unfair. Her conscience told her to speak up next time, kindly but clearly.

Lena

The next morning, Sara realised she did not covet her manager's big office or his loud confidence. What she really craved was respect, and a team where people listen to each other.

Maya

So she changed one thing. In the next meeting, she sat up straight, she did not cross her legs defensively, and she spoke clearly. She conciliated the team with calm words, she defused the old tension with humour, and her conscience felt clear at last.

Lena

That is such a good story, because it shows how the words connect. Conceited, conciliate, conscience, covet, crave, cross your legs, defuse. They are not just vocabulary. They describe how people behave, how they feel, and how they change.

Maya

And that is our episode for today. Remember, a conceited attitude pushes people away, but a calm person who can conciliate and defuse will always be welcome. Listen to your conscience, do not covet what others have, crave the things that truly matter, and sit however you like, as long as you sit with confidence.

Lena

Beautifully said. Thank you for listening to Muse English. Keep practising these seven words, try them in your own sentences, and we will see you tomorrow with seven more. Goodbye for now!

Maya

Goodbye everyone, and keep learning!

📝Grammar: Cleft Sentences (It was... that, What I need is...)

A **cleft sentence** splits one idea into two parts to **emphasise** one of them. *Tom broke the window* becomes ***It was Tom who** broke the window*. There are two main types: **it-clefts** and **wh-clefts**. Both let you put the spotlight exactly where you want it. For an **it-cleft**, put the focus after ***It is / It was***, then add ***that*** or ***who*** for people. *It was the window **that** Tom broke* emphasises the window, not Tom. Use the singular ***It is / It was*** even with a plural focus. *It was my parents **who** paid* is correct, and clefts are perfect for correcting so
