📚 Vocabulary

10 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
sojourn /SOH-jern/
noun
a temporary stay in one place
"During his sojourn in Kyoto, he learned to write haiku."
peregrination /per-uh-gruh-NAY-shun/
noun
a long, wandering journey
"Her peregrination across South America took nearly two years."
peripatetic /per-uh-puh-TET-ik/
adjective
traveling from place to place
"The peripatetic photographer rarely sleeps in the same city twice."
odyssey /OD-ih-see/
noun
a long, eventful journey full of adventures
"Getting the visa was a bureaucratic odyssey that took months."
jaunt /JAWNT/
noun
a short, enjoyable trip
"They took a weekend jaunt to the coast."
itinerary /eye-TIN-uh-reh-ree/
noun
a detailed plan of a journey
"Our itinerary includes three cities in five days."
traverse /truh-VERS/
verb
to travel across or through a place
"The caravan traversed the desert in four days."
wayfarer /WAY-fair-er/
noun
a traveler, especially on foot
"The old inn welcomed tired wayfarers every evening."
globetrotter /GLOHB-trot-ter/
noun
someone who travels widely around the world
"As a seasoned globetrotter, she has visited sixty countries."
layover /LAY-oh-ver/
noun
a stop between flights during a journey
"We have a six-hour layover in Istanbul before the next flight."

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Hello and welcome to Word Kitchen, the show where we cook up new English words and serve them fresh. I am Maya.

Lena

And I am Lena. Maya, today we are packing our bags, because our theme is travel and transport, and we have ten rich, sophisticated words for people who love the road.

Maya

Exactly. Ten words that will make your travel stories sound far more interesting. Shall we start with a personal one? I once had a sojourn in Lisbon. Just six weeks, but those six weeks changed me.

Lena

A sojourn is a temporary stay in one place, right? Longer than a visit, shorter than a move. A little pause in life where you really settle into a place.

Maya

Yes, that is the beauty of a sojourn. You are not rushing. You rent a little apartment, you find your favorite coffee spot, and the city slowly becomes yours. My Lisbon sojourn gave me friendships I still keep today.

Lena

I love that. A sojourn is travel with roots. You unpack your suitcase instead of living out of it. I had a short sojourn in Montreal once, and by the second week the baker at the corner knew my order.

Maya

That is the dream. And speaking of slower, deeper journeys, let me introduce our second word: peregrination. It means a long, wandering journey with no fixed destination.

Lena

Peregrination. That sounds poetic. Like those travelers who just walk and let the road decide.

Maya

Exactly. A peregrination is not about efficiency. It is about wandering with curiosity. The nineteenth-century naturalists went on peregrinations across continents, collecting plants and stories.

Lena

So a peregrination is the opposite of a rushed business trip. You might start a peregrination with a rough direction and end up somewhere you never imagined.

Maya

Right. And honestly, every great gap year is a small peregrination in spirit. You wander, you learn, you grow. My dream peregrination would be along the old Silk Road, slowly, on foot where possible.

Lena

On foot? That makes you a wayfarer, Maya! Our next word. A wayfarer is a traveler, especially one who walks from place to place.

Maya

A wayfarer. I love how old-fashioned and romantic that word sounds. The wayfarer carries a backpack, not a suitcase. The wayfarer stops at small inns and talks to strangers.

Lena

In old stories, the wayfarer is always welcome at the village table. There is something noble about it. I picture a wayfarer crossing mountain passes with nothing but a map and good boots.

Maya

And every modern hiker is a wayfarer at heart. You know, Lena, some people are born wayfarers. They cannot stay in one place for long. They need the horizon.

Lena

That reminds me of our next word: peripatetic. It describes someone who travels from place to place rather than settling down. A peripatetic life.

Maya

Yes. Consultants, touring musicians, travel writers. They live a peripatetic existence. My cousin is a peripatetic photographer, and her address is basically her camera bag.

Lena

A peripatetic lifestyle sounds exciting but exhausting. The peripatetic worker is always in airports, always in hotels. You gain the world but lose your favorite chair.

Maya

True. But some people thrive on it. A peripatetic childhood can make you adaptable and open. Though I must admit, after two weeks of travel, even I stop being peripatetic in spirit and start missing my own bed.

Lena

Ha! Me too. Now, let us talk about journeys that test you. Our fifth word is odyssey. An odyssey is a long, eventful journey, full of adventures and surprises.

Maya

Odyssey. From the ancient Greek story of Odysseus. An odyssey is never smooth. It has storms, detours, and lessons. My friend called her move across three countries an odyssey, because everything that could go wrong did.

Lena

That is the thing about an odyssey. It changes the traveler. You do not come back from an odyssey the same person. The hard parts are the point.

Maya

Exactly. Even a complicated trip can become an odyssey. Missed flights, lost luggage, a bus that breaks down in the desert. At the time it is stressful. Later, it is your favorite story.

Lena

So an odyssey is a journey with a plot. If your travel story has twists and turns, call it an odyssey and people will listen twice as carefully.

Maya

Wonderful. Now let us lighten up with jaunt. A jaunt is a short, enjoyable trip, usually for pleasure. The perfect weekend escape.

Lena

A jaunt! That word sounds like fun. A Saturday jaunt to the countryside. A quick jaunt to the beach with friends.

Maya

Yes. A jaunt is small and happy. You do not need a visa or a big plan for a jaunt. My favorite jaunt this year was a Sunday drive to a vineyard an hour away.

Lena

That is the charm of a jaunt. It is low effort and high reward. Honestly, a little jaunt every month keeps me sane. Work hard, then jaunt a little.

Maya

Agreed. Now, even a jaunt needs a plan, and that brings us to itinerary. An itinerary is a detailed plan of a journey: the route, the dates, the stops.

Lena

An itinerary. The document that turns chaos into a trip. A good itinerary lists your flights, your hotels, your sightseeing days.

Maya

And the great debate: strict itinerary or loose itinerary? I like a loose itinerary. Plan the big things, leave room for surprises. A rigid itinerary kills the magic.

Lena

I am the opposite. A detailed itinerary calms me down. When my itinerary is clear, I can actually relax and enjoy. But I leave one free day in every itinerary for pure wandering.

Maya

That is a smart compromise. And here is a pro tip: always share your itinerary with someone back home. Safety first, especially on solo trips. An itinerary is also useful when friends join you halfway.

Lena

True. Okay, next word. This one is a verb: traverse. To traverse means to travel across or through a place, especially somewhere difficult.

Maya

Traverse. Strong word. Explorers traverse deserts. Sailors traverse oceans. To traverse is to cross with effort and purpose.

Lena

I like how active it sounds. You do not just visit a mountain range, you traverse it. Hikers traverse ridgelines. Trains traverse continents.

Maya

Exactly. And you can use it metaphorically too, but for travel it is perfect. My dream is to traverse Patagonia from north to south. Slowly. On foot.

Lena

On foot again! You really are a wayfarer at heart, Maya.

Maya

Maybe I am. Now, Lena, tell me about the word globetrotter. That one feels like a compliment.

Lena

A globetrotter is someone who travels widely around the world. The seasoned globetrotter has stories from every continent.

Maya

It does sound like a badge of honor. A true globetrotter does not just collect passport stamps. The globetrotter collects perspectives.

Lena

Yes! Every globetrotter I know is a better storyteller. My uncle is a globetrotter, and dinner with him is like a world tour.

Maya

I aspire to that. Though to be a real globetrotter, you need time, money, and curiosity. The curiosity is the most important part.

Lena

Absolutely. And our last word is one every air traveler knows: layover. A layover is a stop between flights during a journey.

Maya

The layover. That strange in-between time at an airport. A short layover is stressful. A long layover is a gift if you use it well.

Lena

I love a long layover. A six-hour layover in a new city is a free mini-trip. I once had a layover in Singapore and saw the whole city in eight hours.

Maya

Smart. The worst is a layover at midnight with nothing open. But even then, a layover is a pause, a breath between journeys. Plan for the layover and it becomes part of the adventure.

Lena

And that is our ten! Let us do a quick review, Maya. I will say the word, you give the meaning.

Maya

Ready.

Lena

Wayfarer.

Maya

A traveler, especially on foot. The romantic walker of the roads.

Lena

Peripatetic.

Maya

Traveling from place to place. The adjective for a life on the move.

Lena

Odyssey.

Maya

A long, eventful journey full of adventures and surprises. The epic trip.

Lena

Jaunt.

Maya

A short, enjoyable trip. The happy weekend escape.

Lena

Someone who travels widely around the world. The world citizen.

Perfect. Listeners, try using these words this week. Tell someone about your last jaunt. Share your dream itinerary. Describe your longest layover.

And if you have had a real odyssey, write to us and tell the story. We might read it on the show.

Before we close, Maya, let us give our listeners a small challenge. Write one sentence with any of these words and post it in the comments.

Great idea. For example, describe your last layover in one sentence. Or write about a dream odyssey you would love to take.

Or tell us your perfect itinerary for a weekend jaunt. I will read every single one, and I will reply to the most creative answers.

And if your sentence uses a word like peregrination or peripatetic correctly, I will be seriously impressed. Show us what you learned today.

That is all for today. Keep wandering, keep learning, and remember: the world is wide, and your vocabulary should be too.

📝Grammar: Present perfect + for / since, present perfect continuous

Use the **present perfect + for / since** for things that started in the past and are still true now. Think of an unfinished state, like a *sojourn* that has not ended. Use **for** with a period of time and **since** with a starting point. A flexible *itinerary* still needs **for** or **since** to anchor it in time. Ask about unfinished periods with **How long + present perfect**. The answer often describes a *peregrination* still in progress. Use the **present perfect continuous (have / has been + verb-ing)** for actions that began in the past and are still happening now. The *peripatetic*
