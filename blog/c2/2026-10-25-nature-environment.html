📚 Vocabulary

7 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
absolute poverty /AB-suh-loot POV-er-tee/
phrase
Lacking basic necessities like food, water, and shelter.
"Absolute poverty remains a global challenge."
affluent /AF-loo-ent/
adjective
Having a great deal of money; wealthy.
"Affluent nations often have higher carbon footprints."
ambush (n. and v.) /AM-bush/
noun, verb
A surprise attack from a hidden position.
"The rebels planned an ambush on the convoy carrying aid."
animal (rescue) shelter /AN-uh-mul RES-kyoo SHEL-ter/
noun
A place providing temporary care for homeless animals.
"We adopted our dog from the local animal rescue shelter."
animal rights activist /AN-uh-mul RYTS AK-tuh-vist/
noun
A person campaigning for the ethical treatment of animals.
"Animal rights activists protested against the fur industry."
appliance /uh-PLY-unss/
noun
A device or machine for a specific purpose.
"Energy-efficient appliances help reduce household carbon footprint."
besiege /buh-SEEJ/
verb
To surround a place with armed forces; overwhelm.
"The city was besieged by enemy forces for months."

Lena

I loved that part. The animal rights activist said that being an animal rights activist is not only about protests. She said every animal rights activist she knows also rescues, fosters, and finds homes for animals, and that an animal rights activist can change minds simply by telling true stories.

Maya

The film also asked a hard question about money. It showed affluent neighborhoods, places where people have a great deal of money, and asked whether affluent families use more resources than everyone else. The answer was uncomfortable, because affluent households often own bigger homes, more cars, and more gadgets.

Lena

That is true, and it connects to something I think about a lot. An affluent lifestyle usually means a bigger carbon footprint, and affluent countries, on average, produce far more waste per person. Maya

Exactly. And the film linked wealth to a much sadder story, which is absolute poverty. Absolute poverty means lacking basic necessities like food, water, and shelter. When people live in absolute poverty, they may cut down forests just to cook dinner, because absolute poverty leaves them no other choice.

That broke my heart. The filmmakers said absolute poverty is one of the biggest threats to wildlife, because absolute poverty pushes families to hunt protected animals for food. If we want to save nature, we have to fight absolute poverty at the same time.

I remember that line. He said climate change can besiege whole regions, and when rising seas besiege a coastal town, families have to leave their homes. It is a powerful image, because to besiege a place is to cut it off completely.

That scene showed why the work matters. The animal rights activist who led the team said her group visits villages before disasters, teaching families how to keep animals safe. She believes an animal rights activist should be practical, not just loud, and her team proved it that day.

Welcome back. Let us connect the pieces. The documentary argued that an animal rescue shelter, an animal rights activist, and ordinary families all play a part. The shelter heals animals, the activist speaks for them, and families choose how they live.

Maya

And the choices matter. An affluent family that buys a smaller car, fixes a leaking tap, and replaces an old appliance shows that wealth does not have to harm nature. Meanwhile, a community fighting absolute poverty needs clean water, schools, and jobs, so that absolute poverty no longer forces people to destroy the forest around them.

Lena

I also keep thinking about the word ambush. The rangers said the best way to stop an ambush is to remove the reason for it, by giving local people jobs as guides and guards. When people earn a living from tourism, nobody needs to ambush wildlife anymore.

Maya

One more idea from the film deserves attention. The filmmakers visited a town where rising rivers threaten homes every spring. Engineers there built barriers, but the narrator warned that water can still besiege a town if the barriers fail. The town now works with an animal rescue shelter to move pets to safety during floods, so no animal is left behind when the water rises.

Lena

That is the kind of planning I admire. And it brings us back to money. Affluent donors paid for those barriers, which shows that affluent people can protect whole communities when they invest in prevention. At the same time, the film reminded us that absolute poverty makes prevention harder, because families facing absolute poverty cannot afford insurance, strong houses, or safe land.

Maya

Exactly. And here is where the words meet real life. If you ever visit an animal rescue shelter, ask what they need most. It might be blankets, food, or simply your time. If you meet an animal rights activist, ask what changed their mind, because their story might change yours too.

Maya

That is a hopeful ending. The film closed with the shelter releasing a rehabilitated elephant back into the wild, and the animal rights activist we met earlier was there, crying with joy. It reminded me that nature can recover, if we give it a chance.

Lena

So here is our recap. Absolute poverty harms people and nature together. Affluent lifestyles carry a bigger footprint, but also a bigger chance to help. An ambush threatens wildlife, while an animal rescue shelter heals it. An animal rights activist speaks up for animals, an appliance can waste or save energy, and a storm can besiege a town. Seven words, one connected story.

Maya

Here is a small challenge for you. This week, notice one appliance in your home and check whether it wastes energy. Maya

Lena

One last thought before we go. The documentary ends with a simple message, which is that every choice counts. The food we buy, the energy we use, and the stories we share all shape the world around us.

📝Grammar: Nominalisation in Formal English

**Nominalisation** means expressing an action or quality as a **noun** instead of a verb or adjective. It is typical of academic, business and news writing, because it packs more information into fewer words and sounds more objective. To nominalise, turn the verb into a noun, and turn any adverb into an adjective: **decided** becomes **decision**, and **rapidly** becomes **rapid**. Common noun endings are **-tion**, **-ment**, **-ance**, **-ity**, **-ness** and **-ure**: **decision**, **development**, **importance**, **awareness**.
