📚 Vocabulary

10 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
hypertrophy /hy-PER-truh-fee/
noun
the growth of muscle tissue that makes muscles larger
"After months of heavy lifting, he noticed real hypertrophy in his shoulders."
plyometrics /plai-uh-MET-riks/
noun
jump-based exercises that develop explosive power
"Her coach added plyometrics like box jumps to sharpen her sprint starts."
proprioception /PROH-pree-uh-SEP-shun/
noun
the body's sense of where it is in space
"Balance-board drills improve proprioception and help prevent ankle injuries."
ergogenic /ur-guh-JEN-ik/
adjective
intended to improve physical performance
"Caffeine is one of the few supplements with proven ergogenic effects."
taper /TAY-pur/
verb
to gradually reduce training before a competition
"She will taper her mileage in the final two weeks before the marathon."
deload /dee-LOHD/
verb
to train lighter for a short period to allow recovery
"After three heavy weeks, he plans to deload with easy sessions."
calisthenics /kal-iss-THEN-iks/
noun
exercises using only body weight, such as push-ups
"Calisthenics like pull-ups and dips built his upper-body strength."
macrocycle /MAK-roh-SAI-kul/
noun
a long-term training plan lasting several months
"His coach mapped the whole season as one twelve-week macrocycle."
neuromuscular /noo-roh-MUSS-kyoo-lur/
adjective
relating to the connection between nerves and muscles
"Sprint training creates powerful neuromuscular adaptations."
eustress /YOO-stress/
noun
positive stress that motivates and improves performance
"The buzz before a race is eustress, not anxiety."

Podcast: The Science of Training

Listen to today’s conversation.

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome back to Word Kitchen, the show where we take ten powerful English words and cook them into real conversation. I'm Maya.

Lena

And I'm Lena. Today's menu is the science of sports and fitness. We have ten advanced words that athletes, coaches, and physiotherapists actually use when they talk about training.

Maya

These are C two level words, so they are precise and sophisticated. If you train hard, or you just want to understand how the body adapts, this episode is for you. Let's start cooking.

Lena

Our first word is hypertrophy. Hypertrophy means the growth of muscle tissue that makes muscles larger. When someone lifts weights to build bigger muscles, they are training for hypertrophy.

Maya

Right, and there is a subtle point here. Hypertrophy is not the same as strength. A beginner can get much stronger in the first month with almost no visible hypertrophy, because the early gains come from the nervous system learning to recruit muscle fibers.

Lena

Exactly. Real hypertrophy takes months of consistent overload plus enough protein and sleep. So if your goal is hypertrophy, you need patience, progressive volume, and recovery. It is a slow biological process, not a two-week transformation.

Maya

Great framing. Our second word is plyometrics. Plyometrics are jump-based exercises that develop explosive power. Think box jumps, bounding, and depth jumps.

Lena

Plyometrics train the stretch-shortening cycle, that spring-like action of muscles and tendons. Sprinters and basketball players live on plyometrics because their sports demand instant, explosive force.

Maya

But a warning from the coaching world: plyometrics are high impact. Doing advanced plyometrics on tired legs or hard concrete is asking for injury. Quality over quantity, a few crisp sets of plyometrics beat twenty sloppy ones.

Lena

Word three is proprioception. Proprioception is the body's sense of where it is in space. Close your eyes and touch your nose. That works because of proprioception.

Maya

Athletes with sharp proprioception move efficiently and avoid injury. After an ankle sprain, rehab is largely about rebuilding proprioception, often with balance boards and single-leg drills.

Lena

I love that. So proprioception is like an internal GPS for your joints. Gymnasts and surfers have extraordinary proprioception, and the rest of us can train it with simple daily balance work.

Maya

Word four: ergogenic. Ergogenic means intended to improve physical performance. Caffeine is probably the most famous ergogenic aid in sport.

Lena

The supplement industry is full of so-called ergogenic products, but very few have real evidence. Creatine and caffeine have solid ergogenic research behind them. Most other ergogenic claims are marketing, not science.

Maya

And at the elite level, the line between a legal ergogenic aid and doping can be razor thin, which is why athletes check every ergogenic product against the banned list.

Lena

Word five is taper. To taper means to gradually reduce training before a competition. Marathon runners taper for two or three weeks before race day.

Maya

Tapering feels wrong psychologically. You have trained for months, and suddenly your coach tells you to taper, to do less. But a proper taper lets fatigue fade while fitness stays, so you arrive fresh.

Lena

The classic mistake is refusing to taper and training hard right up to the event. Trust the taper. The fitness is already in the bank.

Maya

Word six: deload. To deload means to train lighter for a short period to allow recovery. Many programs schedule a deload week every fourth week.

Lena

A deload is not a week off. You still move, you still practice technique, but you deload the intensity and volume, maybe lifting at sixty percent. It is planned recovery, not laziness.

Maya

Lifters who never deload usually hit a wall: stalled progress, achy joints, flat motivation. A smart deload week often leads to personal bests the week after.

Lena

Word seven is calisthenics. Calisthenics are exercises using only body weight, such as push-ups, pull-ups, and dips. No gym required.

Maya

Calisthenics build impressive relative strength. A strict muscle-up demands more coordination than most machine exercises. And calisthenics scale beautifully, from knee push-ups to one-arm push-ups.

Lena

During lockdowns, calisthenics exploded in popularity because all you need is a floor and maybe a pull-up bar. Some of the strongest people I know train mostly calisthenics in a park.

Maya

Word eight: macrocycle. A macrocycle is a long-term training plan lasting several months. Coaches divide the year into one or two macrocycles.

Lena

Inside a macrocycle you have smaller blocks: build phases, peak phases, and recovery weeks. A well-designed macrocycle ends with the athlete peaking exactly at the championship.

Maya

Without a macrocycle, training becomes random workouts. With a macrocycle, every session has a purpose inside a bigger story. That is the difference between exercising and training.

Lena

Word nine is neuromuscular. Neuromuscular relates to the connection between nerves and muscles. Sprinting is the ultimate neuromuscular skill.

Maya

Heavy lifting and sprint drills create neuromuscular adaptations: faster signaling, better coordination, more fibers firing together. That is why those early strength gains we mentioned are neuromuscular, not hypertrophy.

Lena

As we age, neuromuscular power declines faster than muscle size, so explosive, neuromuscular training becomes even more important after forty.

Maya

Our final word is eustress. Eustress is positive stress that motivates and improves performance. The buzz before a race is eustress, not anxiety.

Lena

Hans Selye, the stress researcher, coined eustress as the good twin of distress. A hard workout is eustress for the body: challenging enough to trigger adaptation, but not so much that it breaks you down.

Maya

Reframing nerves as eustress is a genuine performance tool. Studies show athletes who interpret pre-competition arousal as eustress perform better than those who fight it.

Maya

Before the recap, let's put all ten words to work in one story. Imagine Lena is coaching a fictional sprinter, let's call her Sara, through a full macrocycle.

Lena

Love it. Sara's macrocycle is sixteen weeks, ending at the national championships. Weeks one to eight are the base phase: strength work for hypertrophy, plus calisthenics circuits for general athleticism.

Maya

So in the base phase, Sara does her squats and presses for hypertrophy, building the muscle cross-section she will later teach to fire fast. Twice a week she adds low-volume plyometrics, just to keep the elastic qualities alive.

Lena

Exactly, and her coach tests proprioception monthly with single-leg balance tasks, because sprinting at top speed with poor proprioception is how hamstrings tear. Good proprioception keeps her mechanics clean when fatigue sets in.

Maya

Nutrition-wise, Sara keeps it simple. She uses one ergogenic aid with real evidence, caffeine before key sessions, and ignores the exotic ergogenic powders her teammates swear by. Food, sleep, and sunlight do the heavy lifting.

Lena

Weeks nine to twelve shift toward neuromuscular power. Less hypertrophy work, more sprint-specific neuromuscular drills: flying thirties, resisted sprints, and sharper plyometrics. The neuromuscular system learns to express the strength she built.

Maya

Then week thirteen is a deload. Sara will deload the volume by half, keep the intensity moderate, and let her tendons calm down. She has learned that a well-timed deload is what lets the previous twelve weeks actually sink in.

Lena

Weeks fourteen and fifteen, she starts to taper. To taper correctly she cuts volume sharply but keeps a few fast, short efforts so she stays sharp. The taper is where many athletes panic, so we talk about it constantly.

Maya

And psychologically, we reframe everything as eustress. The championship pressure, the hard sessions, even the taper restlessness, it is all eustress, fuel for adaptation, as long as recovery is respected.

Lena

Race week arrives. Sara is rested from the taper, her proprioception is dialed in, her neuromuscular system is firing, and she treats the final nerves as pure eustress. She runs a personal best.

Maya

That story is the whole episode in one athlete: hypertrophy in the base, plyometrics for power, proprioception for safety, one honest ergogenic aid, a planned deload, a brave taper, calisthenics for athleticism, all inside a smart macrocycle, driven by neuromuscular adaptation and healthy eustress.

Lena

One more thing before the recap. We got two great listener questions this week. First: should a recreational lifter ever deload, or is that just for athletes?

Maya

Great question. Anyone training hard three or more days a week benefits from an occasional deload. You do not need a formal macrocycle to use one. When your sleep is fine but your lifts feel heavy for no reason, take a deload week and watch your numbers rebound.

Lena

Second question: are plyometrics safe for beginners? The honest answer is that basic plyometrics like small hops are fine, but advanced plyometrics belong later, after you have built base strength and decent proprioception. Walk before you bound, literally.

Maya

And a bonus tip on eustress versus distress: if your resting heart rate is climbing and you dread training, that is no longer eustress. Back off, deload, sleep. Eustress builds you up. Distress breaks you down.

Lena

Let's do a quick lightning round to lock these in. Hypertrophy is muscle growth from training. Plyometrics are explosive jump drills.

Maya

Proprioception is your body's position sense. Ergogenic means performance-enhancing. Taper is reducing training before competition, and deload is a planned easy week for recovery.

Lena

Calisthenics use only body weight. A macrocycle is a months-long training plan. Neuromuscular describes the nerve-muscle connection. And eustress is the good kind of stress.

Maya

Beautiful. Ten words, one connected story of how athletes train smarter. Use them this week: describe your own training with hypertrophy, taper, deload, and the rest, and they will stick.

Lena

Thanks for cooking with us on Word Kitchen. Train smart, recover smarter, and we will see you in the next episode.

📝Grammar: Articles - a / an, the, no article

Use **a / an** the first time you mention a singular countable noun. It says what kind of thing it is, not which one. Her coach wrote **a** macrocycle for the season. The same **a / an** appears in **What ...!** exclamations and frequency phrases. What **a** neuromuscular performance that was! She lifts four times **a** week. Switch to **the** once the noun is already mentioned or clearly identified. She wrote **a** macrocycle. **The** macrocycle peaks in June. **The** also marks the only one, well-known places, and superlatives. **The** sun, **the** gym on Main Street, **the** best ergogen
