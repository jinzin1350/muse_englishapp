📚 Vocabulary

7 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
technocratic /tek-nuh-KRAT-ik/
adjective
decided or controlled by technical experts rather than by politicians or ordinary people
"The technocratic committee imposed the new data policy without consulting the public."
cybernetics /sy-bur-NET-iks/
noun
the science of communication and automatic control in machines and living things
"Modern cybernetics treats the brain and the computer as variations on the same information-processing system."
techlash /TEK-lash/
noun
strong public anger and backlash against powerful technology companies
"The techlash of the last decade forced governments to confront the power of the platform giants."
luddism /LUD-iz-um/
noun
opposition to new technology, named after the nineteenth-century workers who destroyed new machines
"His refusal to own a smartphone is less luddism than a deliberate choice to protect his attention."
technosolutionism /tek-noh-suh-LOO-shuh-niz-um/
noun
the belief that every social or political problem has a technological solution
"Technosolutionism tempts politicians to fund apps instead of fixing the underlying problem."
dark pattern /DARK PAT-urn/
noun
a deceptive design trick in an app or website that manipulates users
"The checkout page used a classic dark pattern, hiding the cancel button in pale grey text."
accelerationism /ak-sel-uh-RAY-shun-iz-um/
noun
the idea that technological and economic change should be accelerated rather than slowed down
"Silicon Valley accelerationism treats every regulation as an obstacle to inevitable progress."

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome to Muse English! I'm Maya, and with me as always is Lena. Today we are going deep into the culture of technology, the ideas that shape how we think about machines, power, and the people caught between them. Seven words, all advanced level, and we are going to use every one of them over and over until they feel like old friends.

Lena

That is the goal. We will not just define them, we will put them to work in real sentences. Let us start with technocratic. A technocratic government is one run by technical experts. A technocratic decision is one made on data and models, not by elected politicians, and often not by ordinary citizens either.

Maya

The word technocratic carries a cold feeling for me. Imagine a technocratic committee setting your rent with an algorithm, no vote, no debate. Supporters of a technocratic style say experts are rational. They say a technocratic approach avoids the mess of politics, and that a technocratic process is simply more honest than campaigning. Critics say a technocratic process treats people like numbers, and a technocratic answer can still be wrong, just wrong with very precise spreadsheets.

Lena

Think of central banks. Interest rates are set by unelected economists using models most of us will never read. That is technocratic power at its purest. Or think of how social media feeds are tuned by engineers in rooms we will never enter. When a small group of specialists decides what billions of people see every day, that is technocratic rule wearing a hoodie.

Maya

And the tension is real. During a crisis, people often beg for technocratic leadership, just listen to the scientists, they say. But when the crisis passes, the same citizens resent being governed by experts they never chose. So the question we can ask ourselves all week is simple. Who gets a say, the technocratic experts, or the rest of us?

Lena

Now, a very different lens on the same machine world, cybernetics. Cybernetics is the science of communication and control, in machines and in living things. The founders of cybernetics asked how a thermostat, a ship's rudder, and a human brain all stay on course, and they found the same answer, feedback loops.

Maya

I love that cybernetics erases the line between people and machines. A cybernetics view of the body says your nervous system is a control system, sensing, adjusting, correcting. A cybernetics view of the internet says the whole network is a giant feedback machine, where every click teaches it how to keep you clicking.

Lena

Exactly, and that is where cybernetics gets uncomfortable. If everything is feedback, who designs the loop? The engineers with technocratic control design the loop, and suddenly cybernetics is not neutral science anymore, it is a philosophy of power. The recommendation engine that learns your fears and feeds them back to you is pure cybernetics, and it was built by people, not discovered in nature.

Maya

That is a chilling thought to sit with. But let us take a quick pause from philosophy, because we have something practical to tell you.

Lena

We do! If you are enjoying this podcast, you will love the Muse English app. Every day you get new words with real photos and clear pronunciation, this podcast with a full transcript to read along with, shadowing stories that train your speaking, fun quizzes, grammar lessons, and a leaderboard where you earn points. It is free to install, so grab it today.

Maya

Yes, and now back to our seven words. Number three, techlash. Techlash is the wave of public anger against the giant technology companies. The techlash began when people realized their personal data was being harvested and sold, and the techlash grew as regulators in Europe and America finally started asking hard questions and handing out enormous fines.

Lena

The techlash is fascinating because it shows a complete mood change. Ten years ago the same companies were heroes on magazine covers. Then came scandal after scandal, the data leaks, the election manipulation, the mental health research, and the techlash turned them into villains almost overnight. A techlash does not mean people stop using the products, though. Most of us complain about the platforms every morning and then open them anyway. That is the strange heart of the techlash, anger without exit.

Maya

Which leads perfectly to word four, luddism. Luddism means hostility to new technology, named after the nineteenth century English workers who smashed the machines that were replacing them. Calling someone a luddite today is usually an insult, but luddism deserves a fair hearing. Sometimes luddism is just wisdom. When a school bans phones so children can concentrate, is that luddism, or is it common sense?

Lena

I think modern luddism is often a quiet rebellion. The executive who deletes social media from her phone, the village that refuses a giant data center next to its river, the writer who drafts novels on a typewriter, they are all practicing a new kind of luddism, not smashing machines, just stepping back from them. And honestly, after the techlash we just described, a little luddism looks less crazy every year.

Maya

Maybe the healthiest attitude sits somewhere on the spectrum between technocratic faith in experts and pure luddism. Blind trust in the machine is naive, and total rejection is impossible. The skill is knowing when to log on and when to walk away. And speaking of useful skills, we have more coming, but first, a word about the app that brings you these lessons.

Lena

Quick break, friends. The Muse English app is where all of this lives. Daily vocabulary with photos and pronunciation audio, this Muse English podcast with a written transcript, shadowing stories that make your speaking fluent, grammar lessons that finally make sense, quizzes for every lesson, and a points leaderboard to keep you motivated. Download it free today, and bring these seven words into your active vocabulary this week.

Maya

Welcome back. Word five is a mouthful, technosolutionism. Technosolutionism is the belief that every social problem has a technological fix. Traffic? Build an app. Loneliness? Ship a chatbot. A failing school? Hand every child a tablet. Technosolutionism is seductive because it promises change without conflict, and technosolutionism is dangerous for exactly the same reason.

Lena

Give me a concrete example, Maya.

Maya

A city faces a housing crisis. Technosolutionism says build a platform to match roommates, while the real fix is building houses, which costs money and upsets landlords. Technosolutionism lets leaders look busy without spending political capital. It is the technocratic dream in software form, every messy human problem reduced to a product launch.

Lena

And technosolutionism has a partner in crime, our sixth word, dark pattern. A dark pattern is a design trick that manipulates you. The hidden unsubscribe link buried in tiny grey text. The cancel button you can never find. The countdown timer that restarts every time you reload the page. A dark pattern is not bad design, it is design working exactly as intended, against you.

Maya

Everyone has met a dark pattern. You sign up in one click and need a phone call and a supervisor to cancel, that is a dark pattern at work. The cookie banner with a giant glowing accept button and a reject option hidden three menus deep, that is a dark pattern too. The most sinister dark pattern is the one you never notice, the default setting that quietly shares your location with advertisers. Regulators are finally banning the worst dark pattern tricks, which is itself part of the techlash we talked about earlier.

Lena

And our last word, accelerationism. Accelerationism is the idea that change should go faster, not slower. Silicon Valley accelerationism says regulations are just friction, that powerful artificial intelligence should be built as fast as possible and the consequences sorted out later. To an accelerationism believer, pausing is the real danger, because pausing lets rivals win.

Maya

Accelerationism is the mirror image of luddism. Where luddism says slow down, accelerationism says speed up. A technocratic regulator tries to steer a middle course between them, but the accelerationism camp holds most of the money and most of the servers. The deep irony is that accelerationism often uses the tools of cybernetics, feedback loops, optimization, scaling, to escape the very control that cybernetics was invented to study. The science of steering became the science of going too fast to steer.

Lena

That is a beautiful and slightly terrifying thought to end on. But wait, Maya, after all of this, where do you actually stand? Are you closer to the luddism camp or the accelerationism camp?

Maya

Honestly, I move between them every day. I admire the precision of a technocratic solution when it works, and I feel the pull of cybernetics as a way to understand these systems. But I also feel the techlash in my own habits. I catch myself resenting the dark pattern that kept me subscribed to something for a year, and I recognize the technosolutionism in every product launch that promises to fix loneliness with an app.

Lena

Same here. I think most thoughtful people are living in that tension now, suspicious of technocratic promises, allergic to dark patterns, drawn to a little luddism on weekends, yet unwilling to give up the tools. Maybe the real skill of our century is holding all seven of these words in your head at once and still making a clear decision.

Maya

Well said. So let us recap our seven. A technocratic system trusts experts over citizens. Cybernetics studies control and feedback in machines and minds. A techlash is public fury at the tech giants. Luddism resists the new machines. Technosolutionism believes every problem needs an app. A dark pattern is design that tricks you. And accelerationism wants everything faster.

Maya

Use them this week. Notice the technocratic tone of a policy announcement, the dark pattern hiding in your next checkout page, the technosolutionism inside a startup pitch, the quiet luddism of a friend who went offline for a month. The culture of technology is everywhere once you have the words for it.

Lena

📝Grammar: Fronting and Word Order for Emphasis

**Fronting** means moving part of a sentence to the **beginning** for emphasis or dramatic effect. Instead of "A castle stood on the hill," a novelist writes "**On the hill stood** a castle," and the image lands much harder. After a fronted place or direction, a short verb like **be**, **stand**, **lie**, **come**, or **go** can come **before a noun subject**: "**Into the room walked** a technocratic committee." With a pronoun subject, keep normal order: "Here **it comes**," never "Here comes it." Adjectives, participles, **so**, and **such** can also be fronted, with inversion of the verb a
