7 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
abject poverty /AB-jekt POV-er-tee/
phrase
The state of being extremely poor and without hope.
"Millions live in abject poverty worldwide."
achieve economic growth /uh-CHEEV ee-kuh-NOM-ik GROHTH/
phrase
To increase a country's wealth and prosperity.
"Governments aim to achieve economic growth."
ailing /AY-ling/
adjective
In poor health or condition; failing.
"The ailing economy needs urgent intervention."
alleviate debt /uh-LEE-vee-ayt DET/
phrase
To make debt less severe or burdensome.
"The new policy aims to alleviate debt for students."
allocate funds /AL-uh-kayt FUNDZ/
phrase
To distribute money for a particular purpose.
"The government will allocate funds for education."
APR /ay-pee-AR/
abbreviation
Annual Percentage Rate; the yearly cost of borrowing money.
"Always check the APR before taking out a loan."
boost the economy /boost thee ee-KON-uh-mee/
verb phrase
To improve the economic situation.
"Government spending aims to boost the economy."

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome to Muse English! I'm Maya.

Lena

And I'm Lena. Today's topic is money and markets — but not the kind of money you count in your wallet. We're going big: how whole countries get rich, how they stumble, and why the rest of us should understand what's happening.

Maya

Right, because even if you never studied economics, these ideas quietly shape your rent, your groceries, and your future. So let's start with the hardest phrase of all: abject poverty.

Lena

Abject poverty. It's a heavy phrase. It doesn't mean being a little short on cash at the end of the month. It means the state of being extremely poor and without hope — no clean water, no school, no way out that you can see.

Maya

Exactly. And here's the uncomfortable truth: in the same world where some neighborhoods have food delivery apps, other communities still live in abject poverty.

Lena

That's the paradox of our time. Some say the answer is simple: countries need to achieve economic growth. If the whole pie gets bigger, everyone gets a bigger slice.

Maya

Achieve economic growth — increase a country's wealth and prosperity. It sounds obvious. But the hard question is how: open your markets, protect local industries, or bet everything on technology?

Lena

And it gets even trickier, because growth doesn't fix everything by itself. A country can achieve economic growth while millions of its own citizens remain in abject poverty. The numbers look great; the streets don't.

Maya

That happened in several oil-rich countries. They managed to achieve economic growth year after year, but the wealth stayed at the top. So the lesson is: growth matters, but how you share it matters just as much.

Lena

Which brings us to the countries on the other side of the story — the ailing ones.

Maya

Ailing. In poor health or condition; failing. An ailing economy is like a sick patient: exports dropping, factories closing, people losing jobs.

Lena

Europe had a few ailing economies after the last financial crisis. And the doctors — well, the economists — prescribed some very bitter medicine.

Maya

Before we go there, a quick note. If you like learning English the way we're doing it right now — real conversations, real vocabulary — the Muse English app is built for exactly that. You get daily words with photos and pronunciation, this podcast, shadowing stories where you practice speaking, quizzes, grammar lessons, and a leaderboard where you collect points. It's free, and it takes about ten minutes a day.

Lena

And honestly, that's the fun part. You listen to us talk about things like abject poverty and ailing economies, and then the app turns all of it into practice you can actually do. Install it free and see how fast your vocabulary grows.

Maya

Okay, back to the ailing patient. So the economy is ailing. What do governments do first? Usually, they look at debt. Because an ailing economy often comes with a mountain of it.

Lena

Debt that the government owes, debt that businesses owe, debt that families owe. And the big verb we need today is to alleviate debt — to make debt less severe or burdensome.

Maya

To alleviate debt. Imagine you owe so much that you can never catch up. Then the lender says: we will cut what you owe in half. That would alleviate debt immediately. You can breathe again.

Lena

On the personal level, governments sometimes try to alleviate debt for students — forgive a portion of their loans so young people can start their lives. It's controversial, but it shows how powerful the idea is.

Maya

On the country level, it's the same idea but bigger. International lenders sometimes agree to alleviate debt for the poorest nations, so those countries can spend money on schools instead of interest payments. That can be the difference between hope and despair — between a future and continued abject poverty.

Lena

And here's where it connects to our next phrase: allocate funds. To distribute money for a particular purpose.

Maya

Every budget is a choice about how to allocate funds. If a government collects a certain amount in taxes, it has to allocate funds somewhere: hospitals or highways, schools or stadiums.

Lena

And people fight over these decisions! Should we allocate funds to the military, or to research? Should a city allocate funds to public transport so workers can get to their jobs?

Maya

One economist I read said that the smartest countries allocate funds the way a good farmer plants seeds — not where the need is loudest, but where the return will be greatest. A little controversial, but you see the logic.

Lena

It's also a personal word. You can allocate funds in your own life. Some families allocate funds each month: a bit for rent, a bit for food, a bit for savings. The word works at every scale.

Maya

Speaking of personal finance, let's talk about the abbreviation that confuses almost everyone: A P R.

Lena

A P R — Annual Percentage Rate. The yearly cost of borrowing money. If you take a loan, the A P R tells you how much extra you will pay over a year.

Maya

Two credit cards can look identical, but one has an A P R of nineteen percent and the other has an A P R of twenty-six. That difference can cost you thousands.

Lena

And here's the trap: some loans advertise a low monthly payment but hide a brutal A P R. Always check the A P R before taking out a loan — that is genuinely one of the most useful sentences in all of personal finance.

Maya

I wish someone had taught me that at eighteen. I signed up for a store card because they offered me a discount, and the A P R was absolutely shocking. Lesson learned the expensive way.

Lena

We've all been there. And the A P R matters at the national level too, in a way. When a country's economy is ailing, investors demand a higher return to lend it money — which is like a high A P R for the whole country. Painful.

Maya

That connects beautifully to our last phrase: to boost the economy. To improve the economic situation.

Lena

To boost the economy. Governments love this phrase. Cut taxes to boost the economy. Build bridges to boost the economy. Lower interest rates to boost the economy.

Maya

But does it work? Sometimes. When leaders try to boost the economy with big spending, it can create jobs fast. The question is always whether the boost lasts — or whether it just creates a bubble that pops later.

Lena

Central banks try to boost the economy by making borrowing cheaper. But remember our A P R discussion — cheaper for whom? Big companies benefit first; ordinary families feel it much later, if at all.

Maya

And here's a deeper point. You can try to boost the economy all you want, but if you don't also alleviate debt for the people who are drowning in it, the boost stays at the top. The rich get richer; everyone else stays stuck.

Lena

Exactly. That's why these words belong together. Let me put them side by side. To achieve economic growth is the dream. To boost the economy is the attempt. But an ailing system needs more than slogans — it needs someone to alleviate debt and wisely allocate funds, or the growth never reaches the people living in abject poverty.

Maya

That's a perfect summary. And notice the A P R sitting quietly in the middle of it all — the small, personal version of the same story. Interest rates shape nations and households alike.

Lena

Now, a second quick note before we wrap up. The Muse English app takes everything we just talked about and turns it into a daily habit. Seven new words a day with photos, pronunciation you can shadow, these podcasts with full transcripts, grammar lessons, quizzes, and points on a leaderboard to keep you going. It's completely free — install it today and your future self will thank you.

Maya

Couldn't have said it better. Alright, let's go deeper, because at this level you deserve the nuance. Let's talk about the debate that splits economists in half.

Lena

The big one: should governments try to boost the economy during a crisis, or step back and let the market fix itself? If you ask ten economists, you get eleven answers.

Maya

The interventionists say: when the patient is ailing, you treat the patient. Spend money, create jobs, alleviate debt for homeowners, allocate funds to small businesses. Act fast.

Lena

The skeptics say: every time you try to boost the economy artificially, you keep zombie companies alive, punish savers, and make the next crisis worse.

Maya

History gives ammunition to both sides. After the big crash, some countries moved aggressively to alleviate debt and allocate funds to recovery programs — and they bounced back. Others did nothing, and their ailing industries just stayed ailing for a decade.

Lena

But there's a darker reading too. Some critics argue that the push to achieve economic growth at any cost is exactly what creates the next collapse. Growth built on cheap borrowing — growth where nobody checks the A P R — is a house of cards.

Maya

That's the sophisticated view. Not all growth is healthy. If a country manages to achieve economic growth by destroying its rivers and burning its forests, was it really growth? Or was it just theft from the future?

Lena

And who gets the growth? This is the question that never goes away. When leaders promise to boost the economy, working families hear: will my wages rise? Too often the boost flows upward.

Maya

Which is why the phrase abject poverty still matters in wealthy countries. In the richest cities on earth, you can walk past abject poverty on your way to a luxury store. The contrast is the story of our century.

Lena

So what would a wise government do? If I were writing the prescription: first, alleviate debt for the households that are crushed by it. Second, allocate funds to education and health — the slow investments that actually compound. Third, keep the A P R honest so ordinary borrowers aren't exploited.

Maya

And fourth — be honest about the ailing parts. A country that pretends its ailing sectors are fine just delays the cure. Face the numbers, fix the structure, and then try to boost the economy from solid ground.

Lena

I love that. It's like medicine: diagnose before you treat. And the diagnosis needs the right vocabulary — which is exactly what we gave you today.

Maya

Let's do our lightning recap. Seven phrases, one story. Abject poverty — the extreme end of having nothing.

Lena

To achieve economic growth — to make the whole country richer.

Maya

Ailing — sick, failing, in poor condition.

Lena

To alleviate debt — to make the burden lighter.

Maya

To allocate funds — to decide where the money goes.

Lena

A P R — the yearly price of borrowing.

Maya

And to boost the economy — to lift the whole situation up.

Lena

Use them this week. Read the news and spot them. You'll be amazed how often they show up once you know them.

Maya

That's it for today. Practice these words in the app, shadow the story, and we'll see you tomorrow with seven brand-new words.

Lena

Bye for now!

Maya

Bye!

📝Grammar: Participle Clauses (-ing, -ed, having done)

A **participle clause** packs a whole idea into a few words. *Opening the door, she saw the mess* means the same as *As she opened the door, she saw the mess* — but it is shorter and more elegant. Three forms do three jobs. **-ing** is active and happens at the same time, or gives a reason: ***Feeling** tired, I went to bed early.* The **-ed** form is passive: ***Built** in 1889, the tower is now a symbol of Paris.* **Having + -ed** shows one action finished before another: ***Having finished** work, she went home.* The passive version is **having been + -ed**: ***Having been warned** about
