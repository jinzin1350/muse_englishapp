📚 Vocabulary

10 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
tenure /TEN-yur/
noun
the length of time a person holds a particular job or position
"She has held tenure at the university for over fifteen years."
nepotism /NEP-uh-tiz-um/
noun
favoring relatives and friends for jobs instead of the most qualified people
"The company introduced strict rules to root out nepotism in hiring."
micromanage /MY-kroh-MAN-ij/
verb
to control every small detail of how employees do their work
"Great leaders trust their teams instead of trying to micromanage them."
meritocracy /mer-ih-TOK-ruh-see/
noun
a system where people advance based on ability and effort, not connections
"She thrived in a true meritocracy, where talent mattered more than connections."
glass ceiling /GLASS SEEL-ing/
noun phrase
an invisible barrier that stops talented people, especially women, from reaching top positions
"Despite her achievements, she kept hitting the glass ceiling at her firm."
burnout /BURN-out/
noun
complete physical and mental exhaustion caused by overwork
"Three months of eighty-hour weeks left him with severe burnout."
onboarding /ON-bor-ding/
noun
the process of welcoming and training a new employee
"Their onboarding program helps new engineers feel at home in two weeks."
headhunt /HED-hunt/
verb
to approach a skilled worker and recruit them for a job
"A rival startup tried to headhunt our lead designer last spring."
remuneration /rih-myoo-nuh-RAY-shun/
noun
a formal word for the pay and benefits received for work
"The contract offered a generous remuneration package."
whistleblower /WIS-ul-bloh-er/
noun
a person who reports illegal or unethical behavior inside an organization
"A brave whistleblower exposed the fraud before it grew worse."

Maya

Welcome back to Word Kitchen, everyone. I am Maya.

And I am Lena. Today's menu is all about work and careers, from landing a job to climbing the ladder.

We have ten powerful words for you today. Each one will help you talk about professional life with confidence, whether you are in an interview or a meeting.

Lena

Let us start with tenure. Tenure is the length of time a person holds a particular job or position.

Maya

Exactly. People usually talk about tenure when they discuss professors, judges, or long-serving managers. For example, a professor might spend six years working toward tenure at a university.

Lena

So tenure means the position is secure, and the person has earned the right to stay there for a long time.

Maya

Right. She earned tenure after publishing three important research papers. Once you have tenure, you can speak your mind without fearing for your job.

Lena

That is a good word to know. Repeat it with us, tenure. Next up, nepotism.

Maya

Nepotism is the practice of giving jobs or advantages to relatives and friends instead of choosing people based on ability.

Lena

That is such a real workplace issue. For instance, a manager hired his own nephew for a senior role, and everyone called it blatant nepotism.

Maya

Exactly. Nepotism damages trust, because skilled employees feel that no matter how hard they work, the top jobs go to someone's cousin or college roommate.

Lena

Many companies now publish their hiring decisions openly, hoping to prove there is no nepotism behind them.

Maya

Remember, nepotism. Say it, nepotism. Now, our third word, micromanage.

Lena

To micromanage is to control every tiny detail of how employees do their work.

Maya

The classic example is a boss who checks every email, rewrites every report, and refuses to let anyone make a decision. Nobody wants to be micromanaged.

Lena

My friend told me her manager used to micromanage her schedule, even questioning her lunch breaks. She left after four months.

Maya

Leaders who micromanage their teams destroy motivation. A good manager sets clear goals and then steps back, instead of trying to micromanage every step.

Lena

Good advice. Micromanage, micromanage. Say it twice, like the tutor does.

Maya

Our fourth word is meritocracy. A meritocracy is a system where people advance based on talent and effort, not connections.

Lena

Many startups describe themselves as a meritocracy, where promotions go to whoever delivers the best results.

Maya

But a true meritocracy is hard to build. Some companies claim to be a meritocracy, yet their managers quietly favor people from the same background.

Lena

So a real meritocracy rewards performance. She joined a firm that promised a meritocracy, and she rose to director in five years because of her results.

Maya

Say it, meritocracy. Now our fifth word is a famous phrase, glass ceiling.

Lena

The glass ceiling is the invisible barrier that stops talented people, especially women, from reaching the top positions in a company.

Maya

It is invisible because no rule says women cannot lead, yet they still hit the glass ceiling. Many senior roles go to men even when women have stronger records.

Lena

Several talented managers at her bank kept bumping into the glass ceiling, so they pushed the company to publish promotion data.

Maya

Breaking the glass ceiling takes courage and allies. Repeat it, glass ceiling.

Lena

Word six is burnout. Burnout is complete physical and mental exhaustion caused by too much work and too little rest.

Maya

Burnout is everywhere these days. He worked eighty hour weeks for three months and ended up with serious burnout.

Lena

Doctors say burnout shows up as constant tiredness, cynicism, and mistakes. If you ignore burnout, it only gets worse.

Maya

The best cure for burnout is rest, boundaries, and sometimes a real conversation with your manager. Say it, burnout.

Lena

Our seventh word is onboarding. Onboarding is the process of welcoming and training a new employee.

Maya

Good onboarding matters more than people think. A thoughtful onboarding program can help a new hire feel comfortable in two weeks.

Lena

Her company redesigned its onboarding after many new employees quit in the first month. Now onboarding includes a mentor, clear goals, and weekly check ins.

Maya

So never underestimate onboarding. Strong onboarding builds loyalty from day one. Repeat it, onboarding.

Lena

Word eight is headhunt. To headhunt is to approach a skilled professional and recruit them for a job, often from another company.

Maya

Recruiters headhunt top talent all the time. A rival firm tried to headhunt our lead designer after our product launch.

Lena

Being headhunted is flattering, but the decision is serious. She was headhunted by three companies before choosing the one with the best culture.

Maya

If a recruiter calls, remember you are in a strong position. Say it, headhunt.

Lena

Our ninth word is remuneration. Remuneration is a formal word for the pay and benefits a person receives for their work.

Maya

You will see remuneration in contracts and official documents. The new role offered a generous remuneration package, including a bonus and extra vacation days.

Lena

When negotiating, talk about total remuneration, not just salary, because benefits and stock options are part of the remuneration too.

Maya

Exactly. Fair remuneration shows that a company values its people. Repeat it, remuneration.

Lena

And our final word is whistleblower. A whistleblower is a person who reports illegal or unethical behavior inside an organization.

Maya

A whistleblower often takes a personal risk. In this famous case, a whistleblower leaked documents that exposed a massive fraud.

Lena

Laws protect a whistleblower from being fired for speaking up. Without that whistleblower, the corruption might never have been discovered.

Maya

Every workplace needs the courage of a whistleblower sometimes. Say it, whistleblower.

Lena

What a menu. Let us do a quick review. Maya, give me three words and I will define them.

Maya

All right. Nepotism?

Lena

Nepotism is favoring relatives and friends for jobs instead of choosing qualified people.

Maya

Micromanage?

Lena

To micromanage is to control every small detail of an employee's work, which destroys motivation.

Maya

Burnout?

Lena

Burnout is total exhaustion from overwork. It happens when people work too hard for too long without rest.

Maya

Perfect. My turn to test you. Tenure?

Lena

Tenure is the secure position a professor or employee holds after earning the right to stay long term.

Maya

Glass ceiling?

Lena

The glass ceiling is the invisible barrier that keeps talented people from reaching senior roles.

Maya

Onboarding?

Lena

Onboarding is how a company welcomes and trains a new employee, ideally with a mentor and clear goals.

Maya

Excellent. Now, a quick story to tie it all together. Imagine a talented analyst named Sara.

Sara joined a company that called itself a meritocracy, but she soon noticed nepotism in every promotion round.

Her manager would micromanage her projects, and the stress pushed her toward burnout.

Lena

Maya

Then a headhunter from a rival firm tried to headhunt her, offering better pay and real growth.

Lena

During her excellent onboarding at the new company, she learned that a whistleblower had exposed the old firm's unfair practices, and the regulator forced reforms.

Now Sara has earned tenure in her new role, leads a team, and never micromanages anyone.

That story used every word. Listeners, your turn. Use these words in your own career stories this week.

Try describing your own onboarding, or a time you saw nepotism, or how you avoid burnout.

And remember, whether you are seeking tenure, negotiating remuneration, or hoping for a true meritocracy, words give you power.

Lena

Before we close, let us practice how to use these words in a real interview. Imagine the interviewer asks about your current job.

Maya

You could say, I have held my current position with tenure for six years, but I am ready for a new challenge. That sounds confident and professional.

Lena

Or if they ask about company culture, you could say, I want to work in a true meritocracy, where promotions are based on results.

Maya

And if they ask why you are leaving, be honest but polite. You might say, I felt I was hitting the glass ceiling, and I am looking for a place where I can grow.

Lena

What about the salary question? Try, I would like to discuss total remuneration, including benefits, so we can find a fair agreement.

Maya

Good. If they ask about your management style, say, I never micromanage my team. I set clear goals and trust people to deliver.

Lena

And if they ask how you handle pressure, you could say, I manage my workload carefully to avoid burnout, and I encourage my colleagues to do the same.

📝Grammar: Present perfect + for / since, present perfect continuous

We use **have/has + past participle** with **for** (a period of time) or **since** (a point in time) to talk about something that started in the past and is still true now. This is the classic way to describe job tenure or the remuneration you have earned over the years. We ask about unfinished periods with **How long + have/has ...?**. Never use the present simple here — "How long do you try" is wrong when the effort to break the glass ceiling is still going on. We use the **present perfect continuous** (**have/has been + verb-ing**) for actions that started in the past and are still happen
