📚 Vocabulary

10 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
estranged /es-TRAYNJD/
adjective
cut off from family or friends; no longer in contact after a disagreement
"They became estranged after a bitter argument over the inheritance."
reconcile /REK-un-sile/
verb
restore a friendly relationship after a disagreement
"It took them years to reconcile after the falling-out."
kinship /KIN-ship/
noun
a feeling of deep closeness or connection with someone
"She felt an instant kinship with her host family."
feud /FYOOD/
noun
a long, bitter quarrel between families or groups
"The two families have been locked in a feud for decades."
upbringing /UP-bring-ing/
noun
the way a child is raised and educated
"His strict upbringing shaped his strong work ethic."
nurture /NUR-chur/
verb
care for and help someone or something grow
"Good parents nurture their children's curiosity."
empathy /EM-puh-thee/
noun
the ability to understand and share another person's feelings
"A good listener shows real empathy, not just advice."
patriarch /PAY-tree-ark/
noun
the male head of a family or group
"As the patriarch of the family, he made all the important decisions."
alienate /AY-lee-uh-nate/
verb
make someone feel isolated or cut off from others
"His harsh criticism alienated even his closest friends."
fallout /FAWL-owt/
noun
the bad effects and bad feeling that follow a conflict
"The family is still dealing with the fallout from the divorce."

Podcast: Family & Relationships

Listen to today’s conversation.

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome back to Word Kitchen, the show where advanced English gets practical. I'm Maya.

Lena

And I'm Lena. Today we are talking about family — not the simple stuff, but the real, complicated, beautiful mess of relationships.

Maya

Ten advanced words today, all about family and relationships. Let's start with a heavy one. Have you ever known someone who became estranged from their family?

Lena

Oh yes. My college roommate became estranged from her brother after a huge fight about money. They did not speak for three years.

Maya

Three years. That is what estranged means — cut off, no longer in contact. Sometimes it happens after one terrible fight, but often people grow estranged slowly, almost without noticing.

Lena

The sad thing is that most estranged families never planned it. One missed holiday, one harsh word, and suddenly you realize you are estranged from someone you once loved.

Maya

But here is the hopeful part. Many estranged relatives do find their way back. It takes courage, but the door is rarely locked forever.

Lena

Which brings us to our second word: reconcile. To reconcile means to restore a friendly relationship after a disagreement.

Maya

My aunt and uncle had to reconcile after a bitter falling-out over the family business. It took two years of awkward holidays before they could truly reconcile.

Lena

Reconciling is never easy. You cannot reconcile with one quick apology. To reconcile, you have to show up again and again, until trust slowly returns.

Maya

I love that. And when people reconcile, something wonderful can grow — our next word, kinship.

Lena

Kinship is that feeling of deep closeness and connection. It is the warmth you feel when you truly belong somewhere.

Maya

I felt a real kinship with my grandmother's sisters when I visited them last summer. We laughed at the same stories, and that kinship made me feel rooted.

Lena

Kinship is not only about blood. You can feel kinship with a friend's family, with a community, with anyone who makes you feel at home.

Maya

Exactly. But families are not always warm. Our next word is darker: feud.

Lena

A feud is a long, bitter quarrel — often between families, and sometimes it lasts for generations.

Maya

The classic example is a family feud over land or money. One grandfather refuses to forgive, and the feud continues long after anyone remembers how it started.

Lena

A feud poisons everything. Weddings become battlefields. A single feud can split a family tree right in half.

Maya

And notice the connection: an old feud is often the reason people become estranged in the first place. To end the feud, someone has to be brave enough to reconcile first.

Lena

Our fifth word is upbringing — the way you were raised as a child.

Maya

Upbringing shapes everything. Your upbringing decides which values feel normal, how you argue, even how you apologize.

Lena

My upbringing was strict but loving. My father's upbringing was the opposite, very free, and you can see the difference in our personalities.

Maya

Researchers say your upbringing affects the kind of parent you become. A difficult upbringing can make someone either repeat the pattern or break it completely.

Lena

Next: nurture. To nurture means to care for something and help it grow.

Maya

Parents nurture their children, teachers nurture talent, and good friends nurture each other's confidence.

Lena

I try to nurture curiosity in my own kids. When you nurture a child's questions instead of silencing them, you nurture a lifelong learner.

Maya

And a family that can nurture its members through hard times becomes stronger. To nurture is really to love with patience.

Lena

Beautiful. Our seventh word: empathy — the ability to understand and share another person's feelings.

Maya

Empathy is what makes reconciliation possible. Without empathy, you cannot see the other person's pain.

Lena

A good listener shows empathy instead of rushing to give advice. When you practice empathy with your parents, arguments turn into real conversations.

Maya

I think empathy might be the most important word on our list. Real empathy can end a feud faster than any clever argument ever could.

Lena

Next is patriarch — the male head of a family or group.

Maya

In many traditional families, the patriarch makes the big decisions — where everyone lives, how money is spent, who marries whom.

Lena

My great-grandfather was the patriarch of a huge family. The patriarch's word was law at every gathering, for better or worse.

Maya

Modern families are changing, but the figure of the patriarch still matters in many cultures. And respecting the patriarch does not mean you can never question him.

Lena

Our ninth word: alienate. To alienate means to make someone feel isolated or pushed away.

Maya

Harsh criticism can alienate a teenager in seconds. If you always judge and never listen, you will alienate the very people you love.

Lena

A proud patriarch who never listens can alienate his own children. And a long family feud will alienate cousins who were once best friends.

Maya

The opposite of nurture, really. Where nurture builds bridges, to alienate burns them.

Lena

And finally: fallout. Fallout is the damage and bad feeling that follows a conflict.

Maya

After a divorce, the fallout can last for years. Children feel the fallout at school, in friendships, everywhere.

Lena

A family feud leaves a heavy fallout. But the good news is that the fallout does not have to be permanent. Families can reconcile, and the fallout can slowly heal.

Maya

You know, we got a lovely listener message about exactly this. Sarah wrote that she was estranged from her mother for almost five years after a huge argument about her career choice.

Lena

Five years of silence. That must have been painful for both of them.

Maya

It was. Sarah said the turning point came when her grandmother — the quiet matriarch of the family, really — invited them both to dinner without telling either one.

Lena

Oh, clever grandmother. What happened?

Maya

At first it was awful. Sarah said they sat there like strangers, completely estranged despite being mother and daughter. But then her grandmother started telling stories about Sarah's childhood — the funny parts of her upbringing that they had both forgotten.

Lena

Stories can do what arguments cannot. That is empathy in action.

Maya

Exactly. Sarah said she suddenly felt the old kinship again — not the perfect kind, but a real one. By dessert, they agreed to reconcile, slowly, with no pressure.

Lena

I love that story because it shows how nurture works between adults too. We usually talk about parents who nurture children, but Sarah's grandmother managed to nurture the whole family back together.

Maya

And notice what did not happen. The grandmother did not lecture anyone. She did not take sides in the old feud. She simply refused to let anyone stay alienated.

Lena

That is such an important lesson. When you try to force people to reconcile, you often alienate them further. But when you create space with empathy, reconciliation can happen on its own.

Maya

Sarah also said something wise about her upbringing. She realized that her mother's strict upbringing had made her mother afraid of failure — and that fear was behind the original fight.

Lena

That is empathy again — understanding the upbringing behind someone's behavior. Once Sarah saw that, the old feud looked very different.

Maya

The patriarch of Sarah's family — her grandfather — had passed away years before, and Sarah thinks his absence made everything harder. A strong patriarch can sometimes hold a family together, even imperfectly.

Lena

Or, if the patriarch is too rigid, his absence can finally let everyone breathe. It goes both ways.

Maya

True. The real question Sarah asked us was: how do you reconcile without pretending the past never happened?

Lena

Great question. I would say: you reconcile by naming the fallout honestly. You say, this hurt me, and I want us to nurture something better now.

Maya

And you accept that kinship does not mean agreeing on everything. You can feel deep kinship with someone whose choices confuse you.

Lena

Let's bring it all together with a story. Imagine a family with an old feud. The patriarch refuses to apologize. Two brothers are estranged. The fallout touches everyone at every gathering.

Lena

But then one sister shows real empathy. She decides to reconcile. Slowly, the estranged brothers start talking again, and the old feud begins to fade.

Maya

They nurture the new relationship with patience. The happy parts of their shared upbringing remind them of their kinship. Even the proud patriarch softens when he sees it.

Lena

Nobody feels alienated anymore, because everyone chose empathy over pride. The fallout becomes a lesson instead of a life sentence.

Maya

That is the power of these ten words. From estranged to reconcile, from feud to kinship — language gives us a map for healing.

Lena

Let's do a quick review. To be estranged is to be cut off from someone you love. To reconcile is to restore the relationship.

Maya

Kinship is deep connection. A feud is a bitter, long quarrel. Your upbringing is how you were raised.

Lena

To nurture is to help something grow. Empathy is feeling with others. A patriarch is the male head of the family.

Maya

To alienate is to push someone away. And fallout is the damage left after a conflict.

Lena

Use these words this week. Notice the kinship in your own family. If someone you love is estranged, maybe it is time to reconcile.

Maya

And remember — empathy can end any feud, a thoughtful upbringing helps you nurture others, and no patriarch should ever alienate his family, because the fallout is never worth it.

Lena

Thanks for listening to Word Kitchen. I'm Lena.

Maya

And I'm Maya. See you next time.

📝Grammar: Second conditional — choosing between conditionals

The **second conditional** talks about imaginary situations. The shape is **if + past simple** in the condition, and **would/wouldn't + infinitive** in the result. It describes the present or the future, never the past. *If I had more empathy, I would reconcile with my estranged brother* is a dream about now, not a memory.
