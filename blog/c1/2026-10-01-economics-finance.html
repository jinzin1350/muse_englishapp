📚 Vocabulary

20 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
leverage /LEH-vuh-rij/
verb
to use borrowed money or influence to increase the potential scale of gains (and losses)
"The firm used leverage to buy far more assets than its cash alone would allow."
liquidity /lih-KWIH-duh-tee/
noun
how quickly and easily an asset can be converted to cash without losing value
"Real estate has much lower liquidity than stocks or bonds."
volatility /vah-luh-TIH-luh-tee/
noun
the degree of rapid, unpredictable price changes in a market
"Oil price volatility makes long-term budgeting difficult for airlines."
deficit /DEH-fih-sit/
noun
the amount by which spending exceeds income or revenue
"The government ran a deficit of two percent of GDP last year."
surplus /SUR-plus/
noun
an amount left over after spending; the excess of income over expenses
"The trade surplus reached a record high this quarter."
arbitrage /AR-bih-trahzh/
noun
buying cheap in one market and selling higher in another for a nearly risk-free profit
"Algorithmic traders profit from arbitrage between different exchanges."
speculation /speh-kyuh-LEY-shun/
noun
investment in risky assets in the hope of making a large profit
"Pure speculation drove the meme stock to absurd levels."
collateral /kuh-LAH-tuh-rul/
noun
an asset pledged as security for a loan, seized if the borrower defaults
"The bank demanded collateral before approving the mortgage."
dividend /DIH-vih-dend/
noun
a share of a company's profits paid out to its shareholders
"The company raised its dividend for the fifth consecutive year."
equity /EH-kwih-tee/
noun
the ownership value in a company or asset after all debts are subtracted
"His equity in the startup grew tenfold after the public offering."
bubble /BUH-bul/
noun
a rapid rise in asset prices far above their real value, followed by a crash
"The housing bubble burst in 2008, wiping out trillions in wealth."
downturn /DOWN-turn/
noun
a decline in economic or business activity
"Retail sales fell sharply during the economic downturn."
stimulus /STIH-myuh-lus/
noun
government spending or policy measures aimed at boosting the economy
"The central bank announced a stimulus package to revive growth."
recession /rih-SEH-shun/
noun
a prolonged period of economic decline, typically two quarters of shrinking output
"Economists fear a global recession could begin next year."
austerity /aw-STEH-ruh-tee/
noun
government policies of strict spending cuts intended to reduce deficits
"Austerity measures sparked protests across the capital."
subsidize /SUB-sih-dyz/
verb
to financially support an industry or product so its price falls
"The state subsidizes renewable energy to encourage adoption."
bailout /BAYL-out/
noun
an emergency financial rescue of a failing company or economy
"The airline received a government bailout to avoid bankruptcy."
diversification /dih-vur-sih-fih-KEY-shun/
noun
spreading investments across different assets to reduce risk
"Diversification protected her portfolio during the market crash."
inflationary /in-FLAY-shuh-neh-ree/
adjective
causing or relating to inflation; tending to raise prices
"Printing too much money to fund spending is inflationary."
hedge /HEJ/
verb
to make investments that reduce the risk of financial losses
"Investors hedge against inflation by buying gold."

Podcast: Economics & Finance

Listen to today’s conversation.

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome back to Word Kitchen, everyone. I'm Maya.

Lena

And I'm Lena. Today we're doing something listeners have been asking for: a full episode on the language of money, markets, and macroeconomics. Twenty words that will make you sound like you actually read the financial pages.

Maya

And more importantly, understand them. Economic vocabulary is everywhere, from news headlines to your retirement account. Let's start with a word you hear constantly: leverage. To leverage something means to use borrowed money, or borrowed influence, to increase the scale of what you can do. Imagine you have a hundred dollars, you borrow nine hundred more, and you buy a thousand dollars of stock. That is leverage.

Lena

Powerful and dangerous, right? Because leverage magnifies gains, but it also magnifies losses. It's like a crowbar: a small force moves a huge weight, but if that weight falls back, it falls on you.

Maya

Perfect image. Now a word that sounds like a drink but is really about freedom: liquidity. Liquidity is how quickly and easily you can turn an asset into cash without losing value. Cash itself is perfectly liquid. A house? Not very liquid. Selling one can take months.

Lena

And in a crisis, liquidity is everything. When banks panic, they hoard cash, liquidity dries up, and suddenly even healthy companies cannot borrow. It happened in 2008, and markets still have nightmares about it.

Maya

Which brings us to volatility: the degree of rapid, unpredictable price swings. Tech stocks show high volatility; government bonds show low volatility. For traders, volatility is both the risk and the opportunity.

Lena

Governments live in fear of two opposite words: deficit and surplus. A deficit is what you get when you spend more than you earn, the gap you must borrow to fill. A surplus is the opposite, money left over after all the bills are paid.

Maya

Countries argue endlessly about this. Some say deficits are fine if the money funds productive growth; others say chronic deficits end in tears. Meanwhile Norway sits on a giant surplus from oil, patiently saving for the future.

Lena

Okay, here is a fun one: arbitrage. Arbitrage is the elegant trick of buying something cheap in one market and instantly selling it higher in another, pocketing the difference with almost no risk. Say a stock trades at ninety-nine dollars in London and a hundred in New York; computers exploit that arbitrage in milliseconds.

Maya

Its wild cousin is speculation. Speculation means betting on risky assets in the hope of a huge profit, driven more by hope and momentum than by fundamentals. Speculation built fortunes in the dot-com era and destroyed just as many.

Lena

To borrow safely, lenders want collateral: an asset you pledge as security for a loan. Your house is the collateral for your mortgage. If you cannot pay, the bank takes the collateral.

Maya

If you own shares, you hope for a dividend: a payment companies make to shareholders out of their profits. A dividend is literally the company sharing the pie. Many retirees live on dividend income.

Lena

And the pie itself is equity: the ownership value in a company or an asset after all debts are subtracted. When you buy stock, you buy equity. When your house price rises faster than your mortgage, your equity grows.

Maya

Now, bubbles. Not champagne: a bubble is when prices soar far beyond any realistic value, driven by excitement and speculation. The housing bubble, the dot-com bubble, the tulip bubble of the 1600s. Every bubble pops eventually.

Lena

And when the party ends, you get a downturn: a decline in economic activity. Sales drop, companies freeze hiring, confidence fades. A downturn is the economy catching its breath, or catching a cold.

Maya

Governments hate downturns, so they reach for stimulus: spending or tax cuts designed to restart growth. A stimulus package is like a defibrillator for the economy, a jolt of money meant to get the heart beating again.

Lena

But if the downturn is deep and long, economists call it a recession: technically two consecutive quarters of shrinking output. Recessions cost jobs, and the word alone can frighten markets.

Maya

The opposite medicine from stimulus is austerity: strict government spending cuts aimed at shrinking a deficit. After 2008, several European countries tried austerity. Critics argued that cutting spending during a recession just deepens the recession.

Lena

A gentler tool is to subsidize: to financially support an industry so its prices fall. Governments subsidize farming, solar panels, electric cars. To subsidize is to give someone a boost with public money.

Maya

And when a giant company is about to collapse and take the economy down with it, governments sometimes pay for a bailout: an emergency financial rescue. Banks received bailouts in 2008. The public hated it, but officials argued the alternative was worse.

Lena

Smart investors protect themselves with diversification: spreading money across many different assets so that one disaster cannot wipe them out. Do not put all your eggs in one basket; that is diversification in one sentence.

Maya

Watch out for inflationary policies, though. Inflationary means tending to cause inflation, a general rise in prices. Printing enormous amounts of money to fund stimulus can be inflationary, and then your savings buy less.

Lena

So how do professionals sleep at night? They hedge. To hedge means to make investments that offset the risk of losses. A farmer hedges by locking in a future price for wheat; a fund hedges currency risk with options. A hedge is financial insurance.

Maya

Let us pull it all together with a little story. Picture a young investor. She starts with savings and no leverage, just cash, maximum liquidity. She builds equity slowly in index funds and collects small dividends.

Lena

Then she grows ambitious. She uses leverage to buy property, pledging it as collateral. For a while it works: a surplus of income, rising prices. But speculation creeps in, and she buys into a bubble.

Maya

The bubble bursts. A downturn begins, volatility explodes, and her lender panics about liquidity. Her property slides into a deficit of value against the loan. She stares into the abyss of a recession.

Lena

The government answers with stimulus, cuts interest rates, maybe even arranges a targeted bailout for banks. It debates austerity versus spending, chooses to subsidize key industries, and warns against inflationary excess.

Maya

Our investor survives because she learned diversification and how to hedge. She rides out the storm, and slowly the economy heals. That is the whole cycle, and now you have the vocabulary to describe it.

Maya

Before the recap, let us test these words against real headlines, because that is where C1 learners meet them in the wild. Imagine the news says: "Central bank warns stimulus may turn inflationary."

Lena

Translation: the central bank is saying its own emergency spending could cause rising prices. Notice the grammar there: inflationary is an adjective describing the stimulus itself, not the result. A policy can be inflationary; an economy experiences inflation.

Maya

Here is another: "Hedge funds increased leverage as volatility returned." A hedge fund, despite the name, does not always hedge; many use heavy leverage, borrowing big to chase returns, precisely when volatility, those wild swings, makes borrowing most dangerous.

Lena

I love that irony. And how about: "The bailout came with austerity strings attached." That means the rescue money was conditional on harsh spending cuts. A bailout saves you, but austerity is the price.

Maya

One more, and this one separates intermediate from advanced: "Arbitrageurs profited while speculation punished latecomers." Arbitrageurs, the people who do arbitrage, exploit tiny price gaps with almost no risk. Speculators take big risks on momentum. Same market, opposite skill sets.

Lena

And a classic exam-style sentence: "Unlike a downturn, a recession requires two quarters of contraction." Precise vocabulary matters. A downturn is any decline; a recession is a technical term with a definition. Examiners love that distinction.

Maya

Let us also untangle a pair people mix up: deficit and debt. A deficit is the gap in a single year, spending minus revenue. Debt is the accumulation of all past deficits. A country can run a deficit every year and watch its debt climb, or run a surplus and slowly pay debt down.

Lena

Similarly, people confuse dividend with profit. Profit belongs to the company; a dividend is the slice actually paid out to shareholders. A company can be profitable and still pay no dividend, choosing to reinvest instead.

Maya

And collateral versus equity. Collateral is what the lender can seize; equity is what you actually own. If your house is worth three hundred thousand and you owe two hundred thousand, your equity is one hundred thousand, and the house is the collateral securing the loan.

Lena

Finally, subsidize versus bailout. To subsidize is routine support, like farm payments every year. A bailout is a one-time emergency rescue when collapse is imminent. Subsidies prevent pain; bailouts respond to it.

Maya

Beautiful. These are exactly the distinctions that move a learner from B2 to C1: not just knowing the words, but feeling their boundaries.

Lena

Time for a lightning recap. Leverage: using borrowed money to amplify results. Liquidity: how fast you can turn assets into cash. Volatility: wild price swings. Deficit: spending more than you earn. Surplus: money left over.

Maya

Arbitrage: profiting from price differences between markets. Speculation: risky bets on big wins. Collateral: assets pledged for a loan. Dividend: profit shared with shareholders. Equity: ownership value after debts.

Lena

Bubble: prices far above real value. Downturn: economic decline. Stimulus: spending to restart growth. Recession: prolonged contraction. Austerity: harsh spending cuts. Subsidize: support with public money. Bailout: emergency rescue. Diversification: spreading risk. Inflationary: causing inflation. Hedge: protecting against losses.

Maya

Twenty words, one economy. This week, try describing a headline with three of them, or explain leverage to a friend. That is how vocabulary sticks.

Lena

Thanks for listening to Word Kitchen. I'm Lena.

Maya

And I'm Maya. See you next time.

📝Grammar: Obligation and prohibition: have to, must, should

At C1 level you know the basics. The real skill is choosing the right modal for the right shade of meaning. Use **have to** for external, general rules: banks **have to** hold enough liquidity by law. Use **must** for obligation the speaker feels personally: I **must** hedge this position before the weekend. The classic exam trap: **mustn't** = prohibition (you **mustn't** trade on insider information). **Don't have to** = no obligation, free to choose (you **don't have to** buy equities). **Needn't** is the formal British **don't have to**. With a perfect infinitive it reaches the past: yo
