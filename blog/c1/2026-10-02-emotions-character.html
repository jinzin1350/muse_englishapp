📚 لغت‌ها

۱۰ لغت تازه لغت‌های تازه با معنی فارسی، مثال واقعی و تلفظ.
ambivalent /am-BIH-vuh-lent/
صفت · adjective
having mixed or contradictory feelings about something
دارای احساسات متناقض؛ هم موافق هم مخالف
"She felt ambivalent about the promotion: proud of the recognition, but uneasy about the longer hours."
mercurial /mer-KYUR-ee-ul/
صفت · adjective
changing moods quickly and unpredictably
دمدمی‌مزاج؛ با خلق‌وخوی ناپایدار
"The mercurial director praised the team in the morning and dismissed their work by lunch."
effusive /ih-FYOO-siv/
صفت · adjective
showing strong emotion or enthusiasm openly
پرشور و صمیمانه؛ سرشار از ابراز احساسات
"The audience gave the young pianist an effusive standing ovation."
taciturn /TAS-ih-turn/
صفت · adjective
speaking very little; quietly reserved
کم‌حرف و خویشتن‌دار
"Unlike his chatty colleagues, the taciturn engineer rarely spoke at meetings."
contrite /kun-TRYT/
صفت · adjective
feeling deep guilt and wanting to make things right
پشیمان و نادم؛ عمیقاً متأسف
"He wrote a contrite letter apologizing for his harsh words."
acrimonious /ak-rih-MOH-nee-us/
صفت · adjective
full of anger and bitter hostility
تند و پرخاشگرانه؛ آکنده از کینه و تلخی
"Their acrimonious divorce divided the family for years."
ebullient /ih-BUL-yent/
صفت · adjective
extremely cheerful, full of energy
سرشار از شوق و انرژی؛ بشاش
"The ebullient coach celebrated the victory with the whole team."
aloof /uh-LOOF/
صفت · adjective
distant and uninvolved; not friendly
بی‌اعتنا و گوشه‌گیر؛ سرد و دور
"The new manager seemed aloof, rarely joining lunch conversations."
magnanimous /mag-NAN-ih-mus/
صفت · adjective
generous and kind, especially toward those who wronged you
بزرگوار و گذشتکار
"In a magnanimous gesture, she forgave her rival publicly."
supercilious /soo-per-SIL-ee-us/
صفت · adjective
acting as if you are better than others
متکبرانه؛ با نگاه از بالا به دیگران
"His supercilious tone annoyed everyone at the dinner table."

Maya

Lena

And I'm Lena. Today we're cooking up something a little different, Maya, because our theme is people themselves. How we describe character, moods, and emotions in English.

Maya

Exactly. Ten deliciously expressive adjectives, and I have a feeling some of them will describe people we both know. Let's start with a pair that couldn't be more different. Lena, have you ever met someone who is genuinely torn, pulled in two directions?

Lena

Oh, constantly. My cousin got a job offer in London last year, and she was completely ambivalent about it. Thrilled by the salary, heartbroken at leaving her friends. That is ambivalent to perfection. Feeling two opposite things at the same time.

Maya

That is such a good example. Ambivalent is not the same as indifferent, right? Indifferent means you don't care at all. Ambivalent means you care in both directions and they are fighting each other.

Lena

Yes! I feel ambivalent about social media the same way. I love staying in touch with people, but I hate how much time it steals. So when you catch yourself saying, on the one hand, on the other hand, that is your signal. You are ambivalent.

Maya

And here's a subtle thing. In an election year, you hear pollsters say voters are ambivalent about the candidates. They don't mean voters are apathetic. They mean voters hold strong feelings on both sides and cannot settle. Ambivalent is a very honest word, actually. It describes the real, messy middle.

Lena

It really does. Now let me give you the opposite of steady. Have you worked with anyone mercurial? I mean the boss who is sunny at nine in the morning and a thunderstorm by eleven?

Maya

I had a professor exactly like that. Mercurial is a wonderful word because it captures speed. It is not just moody. It is moods that change fast and unpredictably. One minute he would praise your essay, the next minute he would tear it apart.

Lena

Right, and the word comes from Mercury, the quick planet and the messenger god who was famously changeable. So a mercurial person keeps you on your toes. You are always watching the sky, wondering which weather is coming.

Maya

There's a charm to it, though, if you're honest. Mercurial people are rarely boring. Artists, chefs, visionary founders, they can be wonderfully mercurial. The spark that makes them brilliant is the same spark that makes them stormy.

Lena

True, but try planning a project deadline with a mercurial teammate. I once had a colleague whose mercurial temper meant our Monday meetings could go anywhere. We learned to bring backup plans for our backup plans.

Maya

Oh, I feel that. Now let me throw you a warm one after all that storm. Effusive. When someone is effusive, they pour emotion out freely, like a fountain. Big hugs, long thank you notes, tears at weddings.

Lena

My grandmother is the most effusive person alive. She greets you at the door like you just returned from a war. Effusive praise, effusive hugs, effusive everything. It is the opposite of holding back.

Maya

And in professional life, effusive feedback is a gift. A manager who gives an effusive compliment, not just a flat good job, but real, specific, generous praise, that sticks with you for years. People remember how you made them feel.

Lena

They really do. But here's a cultural note I find fascinating. Being effusive is normal in some cultures and suspicious in others. In my hometown, an effusive stranger is just friendly. In other places, people think, what does this person want? So context matters enormously.

Maya

Such a good point. Now, flip it. What about the person who holds everything in? Taciturn. If effusive is a fountain, taciturn is a deep well with a lid on it.

Lena

I know exactly that person. My uncle is wonderfully taciturn. Family dinners, everyone shouting, and he just sits there quietly, and then delivers one perfect sentence that ends the whole debate. Taciturn people speak little, but when they speak, it counts.

Maya

That's the key. Taciturn is not unfriendly. It is just economical with words. A taciturn colleague might never chat by the coffee machine, but ask them a real question and you get gold. In a world of oversharing, the taciturn person is almost refreshing.

Lena

Absolutely. And writers love a taciturn character. The quiet cowboy, the silent detective. Because when a taciturn character finally speaks, the whole room leans in. It is a powerful dramatic tool.

Maya

Okay, time for the apology word, and it's a beautiful one. Contrite. Not just sorry. Contrite is sorry with a weight to it. You feel the guilt in your bones and you want to make it right.

Lena

I think of a child who broke a vase and comes back with a genuinely contrite face. Not performing, not saying sorry to get out of trouble, but truly, deeply contrite. You can see it in their eyes.

Maya

And in adult life, a contrite apology is rare and precious. Most apologies come with excuses attached. A contrite apology has no excuses. It just says, I was wrong, I feel terrible, what can I do? That takes real courage.

Lena

It does. Actually, there is research on this. Studies show that a contrite public apology restores trust far better than a defensive one. People can smell the difference. Contrite means the pride is gone and the humanity is back.

Maya

Speaking of pride and conflict, let's go dark for a minute. Acrimonious. Say it with me. Acrimonious. It sounds like acid, and that's fitting, because it means bitter, hostile, full of venom.

Lena

Divorce lawyers know this word better than anyone. An acrimonious divorce is not just a sad one. It is one where every meeting becomes a battle. The acrimonious tone poisons everything, even the children's birthdays.

Maya

And it's not only personal. Politics gets acrimonious. Comment sections get acrimonious. I read about a neighborhood dispute over a fence that turned acrimonious for three years. A fence! When respect leaves the room, acrimony walks in.

Lena

I love that. And notice the noun form, acrimony. You can say their debates were marked by acrimony. It is such a precise word for that particular flavor of bitterness, where it's not just anger, it's anger with contempt mixed in.

Maya

Let's lift the mood again, because we need some sunshine. Ebullient. If you're ebullient, you are bubbling over with energy and joy. The word literally comes from boiling. You're boiling with happiness.

Lena

An ebullient crowd at a concert, hands in the air. An ebullient teacher on the first day of school. I think children are naturally ebullient, and life slowly teaches them to turn the volume down, which is honestly a tragedy.

Maya

Ha! But some adults keep it. The ebullient sales rep, the ebullient grandmother at every birthday party. And here's the thing. Ebullient energy is contagious. Walk into a room with one truly ebullient person and your whole day changes.

Lena

It really is contagious. I also notice that ebullient doesn't mean naive. You can be ebullient and still be serious about your work. It just means your default setting is joyful, full volume, all heart.

Maya

Now, the distant one. Aloof. The person who stands apart. Not hostile, not angry, just... separate. Like they're watching the party from behind glass.

Lena

Every office has one. The brilliant colleague who is a little aloof. Eats lunch alone, skips the karaoke night. And people misread aloof as arrogant, but often it's just shyness wearing a suit.

Maya

That's so true. Aloof can be a defense mechanism. Or cultural. Or just a personality setting. The mistake is assuming the aloof person doesn't care. Sometimes the aloof observer notices more than anyone in the room.

Lena

Right. Though sometimes aloof is deliberate. Royalty is trained to be aloof. Some executives cultivate an aloof distance on purpose, to keep authority. It's a strategy as much as a trait.

Maya

Okay, my favorite word of the day might be next. Magnanimous. It literally means great-souled. A magnanimous person is generous in victory, forgiving when they don't have to be.

Lena

The classic example. A champion who loses and gives a magnanimous speech praising the winner. No bitterness, no excuses. That is magnanimous, and it is genuinely rare.

Maya

It is. And in everyday life, the magnanimous move is the small one. The colleague who shares credit. The neighbor who forgives the noise complaint. A magnanimous gesture costs you nothing and buys you everything.

Lena

I think magnanimous is the word we should all practice. Imagine if negotiations were magnanimous instead of acrimonious. Imagine a magnanimous response to criticism instead of a defensive one. The world would be a calmer place.

Maya

Beautifully said. And now, the last word, and it's a spicy one. Supercilious. If someone is supercilious, they look down their nose at you. Raised eyebrow, cold smile, the full I'm-better-than-you package.

Lena

The supercilious waiter. The supercilious professor who makes you feel small for asking a question. Supercilious comes from the Latin word for eyebrow, supercilium, which tells you everything. It's all in the eyebrow.

Maya

I love that detail. And the difference between supercilious and merely confident is kindness. A confident expert lifts you up. A supercilious one pushes you down to lift themselves. Nobody forgets how a supercilious comment made them feel.

Lena

Nobody does. And here's a warning for our listeners. When you're learning advanced vocabulary, it's tempting to use a word like supercilious to sound smart, and accidentally sound supercilious yourself. Use the word, don't become the word.

📝گرامر: Ability and possibility: can, could, be able to

Use can for present ability or general possibility. She can read a mercurial boss like a book.

Use could for past ability. He could charm any room before the burnout set in.

Could also softens a present suggestion. We could ask the taciturn analyst; he listens more than he speaks.

Could have + past participle describes a possibility that never happened. They could have avoided the acrimonious split.

Can and could have no infinitive or future forms. Fill the gap with be able to. I would love to be able to stay magnanimous under pressure.
