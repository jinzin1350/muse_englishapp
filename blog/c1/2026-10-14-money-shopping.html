📚 Vocabulary

10 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
commission /kuh-MISH-un/
noun
a fee paid to someone for making a sale, usually a percentage of the price
"The real estate agent earns a six percent commission on every house she sells."
underwrite /UN-duhr-ryt/
verb
to accept financial risk for a deal, as a bank or insurer backing an agreement
"The bank agreed to underwrite the company's new share issue."
monopoly /muh-NOP-uh-lee/
noun
complete control of a market by one company, with no real competition
"The government broke up the telecom monopoly to encourage competition."
oligopoly /ol-ih-GOP-uh-lee/
noun
a market controlled by a small number of large companies
"The airline industry is an oligopoly dominated by three carriers."
procurement /pruh-KYOOR-munt/
noun
the process of buying goods or services for a company or government
"She manages procurement for a hospital, ordering everything from bandages to beds."
liquidation /lik-wih-DAY-shun/
noun
selling off a company's assets to pay its debts, usually when it closes down
"After the liquidation, the shop's remaining stock was auctioned off."
remittance /rih-MIT-uhns/
noun
money sent by a worker abroad back to family in their home country
"Her father's remittances paid for the family's house in Manila."
deflation /dih-FLAY-shun/
noun
a general fall in prices over time, often caused by weak demand
"Economists worry that deflation will make consumers postpone spending."
escrow /ES-kroh/
noun
money held by a neutral third party until the conditions of a deal are met
"The buyers placed their deposit in escrow until the inspection was complete."
fiduciary /fih-DOO-shuh-ree/
adjective
relating to a legal duty to act in someone else's financial interest
"Financial advisers have a fiduciary duty to put their clients first."

But not every commission story is so sweet. My uncle once bought insurance from an agent who pushed the most expensive plan, purely because the commission was higher.

Maya

Right, so the same commission can help or hurt. The trick is knowing when someone's advice is powered by commission and when it's genuinely for you.

Lena

Exactly. So lesson one of our money episode: ask who earns a commission from your decision.

Maya

Okay, back to money. Let's zoom out from the shop floor to the whole market. What happens when one company gets too powerful?

Lena

You get a monopoly. One giant player, no real competition, and prices creep up while quality slides down.

Maya

Classic monopoly. Think of a town where only one internet provider exists. They know you can't leave, so the monopoly sets the rules.

Lena

And then there's the sneaky cousin: an oligopoly. That's when just a few big companies control everything. Not one king, but a small club.

Maya

Right, an oligopoly looks competitive from the outside, but the members quietly move together. Airlines do this. Three big carriers, same fees, same tiny legroom.

Lena

So a monopoly is one giant, an oligopoly is a small group. Both hurt the shopper, and both are worth recognizing.

Maya

Now, on the buyer side, companies have their own money word: procurement. Big firms don't just shop. They run a whole procurement process.

Lena

Procurement means buying at scale, with contracts, tenders, supplier audits. My sister works in procurement for a hospital chain.

Maya

She once told me their procurement team saved the hospital two million dollars in a single year, just by renegotiating glove suppliers.

Lena

That's procurement done right. But sloppy procurement is how companies overpay for years without noticing.

Maya

True. Good procurement is invisible. Bad procurement shows up in the price tag you pay.

Lena

Okay, darker topic. What happens when a business collapses? The word is liquidation.

Maya

Liquidation is the end of the road. The company sells everything: shelves, trucks, stock, to pay its debts.

Lena

I walked past a shop last month with a giant sign: everything must go. That was a liquidation sale. Sad, but the discounts were real.

Maya

A liquidation auction can be a bargain hunter's dream. The store loses, you win. Still, behind every liquidation are people losing jobs, so it's bittersweet.

Lena

And lenders? When a bank backs a risky project, they underwrite it.

Maya

To underwrite means to accept the financial risk. An insurer underwrites your health plan. A bank underwrites a new bond issue.

Lena

My friend's startup needed a bank to underwrite their expansion loan. Without that signature, no money flows.

Maya

Exactly. Someone has to underwrite the risk, or nothing ambitious ever gets funded.

Lena

Now, money crossing borders: remittance. Millions of families live on remittances.

Maya

A remittance is money sent home by someone working abroad. My cousin in Dubai sends a remittance to his parents every single month.

Lena

Those remittances pay for school fees, medicine, new roofs. For some countries, remittance income is bigger than foreign investment.

Maya

It's personal economics at its best. One worker, one remittance, one family lifted.

Maya

You know, Lena, there's a pattern in everything we just covered. Commission, monopoly, oligopoly, procurement, all of them shape the price you pay before you even enter the store.

Lena

That's so true. Think about it: a monopoly raises the price at the top. An oligopoly keeps prices high through quiet agreement. Bad procurement wastes money inside the company, and guess who pays for that? We do.

Maya

And the commission your salesperson earns is baked into that price too. It's all one chain, from the factory to your shopping bag.

Lena

Meanwhile a remittance chain works in reverse. Money flows from the worker back to the family, skipping the middlemen entirely.

Maya

No monopoly there, no commission skimming. Just a direct remittance from one person to the people they love.

Lena

Which makes you wonder: what would shopping look like if every price tag showed its whole story? The procurement cost, the commission, the markup.

Maya

Radical transparency. Some apps are starting to do it. But most stores prefer you never ask.

Lena

Well, we ask. That's what this episode is for.

Lena

Back to the money maze. Ever heard of deflation? It's the opposite of inflation, and it sounds nice until you look closer.

Maya

Deflation means prices fall across the economy. Great for shoppers in the short term, terrifying for businesses.

Lena

During deflation, people delay buying because they think prices will drop further. That kills demand, which deepens the deflation. Nasty spiral.

Maya

Japan fought deflation for decades. Central banks fear deflation almost more than inflation.

Lena

Now a calmer word: escrow. When you buy a house, your deposit sits in escrow.

Maya

Escrow is money held by a neutral third party. Buyer and seller don't trust each other yet, so the escrow agent holds the funds until every condition is met.

Lena

I used an escrow service when I bought my car from a private seller. The money stayed in escrow until the paperwork cleared. Zero stress.

Maya

Escrow is basically trust, outsourced. And our last word: fiduciary.

Lena

Fiduciary is a big one. A financial adviser has a fiduciary duty to act in your best interest, not theirs.

Maya

That's the law in many places. A fiduciary relationship means loyalty is mandatory, not optional.

Lena

So when your adviser recommends a product, ask: are you acting as my fiduciary right now? It changes the whole conversation.

Maya

Before the recap, Lena, let's give our listeners three detective questions for real life.

Lena

I love this. Question one: who earns a commission here? Ask it at the car dealership, the phone store, anywhere.

Maya

Question two: is this a monopoly or an oligopoly? If one company owns the whole street, your bargaining power is zero.

Lena

Question three: is my adviser a fiduciary? If they won't say yes clearly, their advice is advertising.

Maya

And a bonus for the brave: when a shop announces liquidation, go early for the bargains, but go kindly. Real people are behind that sign.

Lena

Beautiful. Money detective badge earned.

Maya

Let's do a lightning recap, Lena. Commission?

Lena

The fee a seller earns on a sale. Ask who earns the commission before you buy.

Maya

Monopoly and oligopoly?

Lena

One giant controlling a market, or a small club of giants. Both raise prices.

Maya

Procurement?

Lena

How big organizations buy. Good procurement saves millions.

Maya

Liquidation?

Lena

Selling everything when a company dies. Bargains with a sad story.

Maya

Underwrite?

Lena

Accepting financial risk so projects can happen.

Maya

Remittance?

Lena

Money sent home from abroad. Lifeline for millions of families.

Maya

Deflation?

Lena

Prices falling economy wide. Sounds good, usually isn't.

Maya

Escrow?

Neutral third party holding money until a deal is done.

And fiduciary?

A legal duty to put the client's interest first. Demand it from your adviser.

📝Grammar: Cleft Sentences and Emphatic Do

In a normal English sentence, the subject comes first and the message flows evenly. A cleft sentence splits one idea in two to shine a spotlight on a single part. Use **It is / was ... that / who ...** to emphasize the subject, the object, or even an adverbial. The spotlighted part sits between is/was and that. Use **What ... is / was ...** (a pseudo-cleft) to emphasize an action or a whole idea. The what-clause builds suspense before the reveal.
