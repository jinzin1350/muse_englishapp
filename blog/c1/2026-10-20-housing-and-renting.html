📚 Vocabulary

7 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
breathe new life into /BREETH noo LYF IN-too/
idiom
To bring new energy or ideas to something.
"The new manager hopes to breathe new life into the team."
council estate /KOWN-sil e-STAYT/
noun
An area with houses built by the local council.
"Many families live on the large council estate."
council housing /KOWN-sil HOW-zing/
noun
Houses provided by the local authority at low rent.
"There's a long waiting list for council housing."
furnished /FUR-nisht/
adjective
(Of a property) rented with furniture included.
"We're looking for a furnished apartment to rent."
get a new lease of life /get uh NYOO LEESS uhv LYF/
idiom
To become healthy, active, or interesting again.
"The old house got a new lease of life after renovation."
granny flat /GRAN-ee FLAT/
noun
A small apartment attached to a house for an elderly relative.
"They built a granny flat for her aging mother."
dog’s life /DOGZ LYF/
idiom
A very unhappy and difficult existence.
"After losing his job, he felt he was living a dog's life."

Podcast: Housing and Renting

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome to Muse English! Today we're talking about home, housing, and how a new home can give you a completely fresh start.

Lena

And about all the different ways people live — from council estates to granny flats, from furnished city flats to a life of your own.

Maya

Let's start with something hopeful. When you breathe new life into something, you give it fresh energy — like it starts over.

Lena

Exactly. Think of an old house that's been empty for years. The windows are broken, the garden is wild... Then someone moves in and decides to breathe new life into it. New paint, new furniture, and suddenly the place is alive again.

Maya

Or a person! Sometimes people move to a new city and the change breathes new life into their whole routine. A new home can breathe new life into a marriage, or a career, or even just a weekend.

Lena

I love that idea — that a building can breathe new life into you, and you can breathe new life into a building. It works both ways.

Maya

Right. My uncle bought a little cottage two years ago. It was in terrible shape, but he worked on it every weekend, and he really breathed new life into the old place. Now it's beautiful.

Lena

And that brings us to a lovely English idiom: to get a new lease of life. A lease is a rental contract, so getting a new lease of life is like signing a fresh contract with life itself.

Maya

Beautiful. So when my uncle breathed new life into the cottage, the cottage got a new lease of life too.

Lena

Exactly. And people get a new lease of life from all kinds of changes. A friend of mine got a new lease of life when she started gardening — her flat was tiny, but the balcony garden gave her a new lease of life.

Maya

Hmm, that's poetic. A new lease of life can come from something small, like learning a skill, or something big, like a new home. The important thing is the feeling of a fresh beginning.

Lena

And speaking of housing in Britain, let's talk about council housing. Council housing is public housing — homes built and owned by local councils, rented at lower prices to people who need them.

Maya

Yes, and a council estate is a group of these homes built together — sometimes whole neighbourhoods of flats and houses, all council housing.

Lena

Some council estates have a difficult reputation, but many people grew up happily on a council estate. My colleague grew up on a council estate in Manchester and says the community there was wonderful — neighbours helping each other, kids playing outside.

Maya

That's the reality for millions. Council housing gave working families a decent place to live when they couldn't afford private rent. Without council housing, many would have lived a dog's life.

Lena

Oh, that's a fun phrase — to live a dog's life means to live a very hard, unhappy life. If you work twelve hours a day in a cold, damp room, you're living a dog's life.

Maya

Right! It's from the idea that dogs were treated badly in the past. So nobody wants to live a dog's life. Decent housing — whether it's council housing or a private flat — means you don't live a dog's life.

Lena

But sometimes even people in nice houses can feel like they're living a dog's life if the job is terrible or the relationship is awful. It's not only about housing.

Maya

True. Anyway — before the break, quick reminder from us at Muse English!

Lena

If you're enjoying today's episode, take one minute and explore the Muse English app. You get daily lessons like this one, pronunciation practice, shadowing stories, and quizzes — all built around real, useful vocabulary.

Maya

And it's free to start. Search "Muse English" in your app store and start today's lesson right now. You'll be breathing new life into your English in no time.

Lena

Welcome back! Now let's talk about granny flats — I love this word.

Maya

Me too. A granny flat is a small separate home built in the garden of a bigger house, or as part of the same building. Originally for a grandmother — hence "granny" — but now anyone can live in a granny flat.

Lena

They're very popular in Australia and parts of America. You build a granny flat next to your house, and your mother-in-law moves in, or a student rents the granny flat. It gives everyone privacy.

Maya

And in a way, a granny flat can breathe new life into a family. Instead of putting an elderly parent in a care home, they get a granny flat of their own — independence plus family nearby. That can give the whole family a new lease of life.

Lena

Absolutely. Now, what about furnished? A furnished flat comes with furniture — beds, tables, chairs, everything. An unfurnished flat is empty; you bring your own.

Maya

Furnished flats are perfect for short stays. If you rent for six months in a new city, you want it furnished — you don't want to buy a sofa and then sell it again.

Lena

And a granny flat is often rented furnished, too, because it's small. Everything the tenant needs is already there.

Maya

Here's an interesting contrast: council housing is usually unfurnished — tenants bring their own furniture. But some council housing for emergency cases comes furnished, so a family with nothing can move straight in.

Lena

That's a nice detail. So — council housing, council estates, granny flats, furnished flats — England and many countries have a whole vocabulary of housing.

Maya

Let's put it all together with a story. Imagine this: Sarah lives on a council estate in London. She works two jobs, and honestly, she feels like she's living a dog's life — no time for herself, no energy.

Lena

Then her grandmother dies and leaves her a small house in the countryside. It's old and falling apart. But Sarah decides to move there and breathe new life into it.

Maya

She sells most of her things, moves into the one room that's still livable, and starts working on the rest. Slowly, the house — and Sarah — get a new lease of life.

Lena

Meanwhile, she rents out a little granny flat at the back of the property, furnished, to a retired teacher. The rent pays for the repairs.

Maya

Six months later, you wouldn't recognise the place. The house has a new lease of life, the granny flat is lovely and furnished, and Sarah no longer lives a dog's life. She gardens, she sleeps well, she's happy.

Lena

That's the dream! And notice — she moved OUT of the council estate, but she says she'd never criticise council housing. The council housing gave her family a stable home when she was a child. She's grateful for it.

Maya

It's a good story because it shows how housing shapes a life. Whether it's a council estate, council housing, a granny flat, or a furnished flat — where we live changes how we feel.

Lena

And sometimes the change is the other way: a house breathes new life into a person. Sarah breathed new life into the house, and the house gave her a new lease of life.

Maya

Let me also say — before our second break — that these idioms are really common in British English. "To get a new lease of life" appears in newspapers constantly: "The old theatre has got a new lease of life," "The neighbourhood has been given a new lease of life."

Lena

And "breathe new life into" too. Politicians love it: "We will breathe new life into the city centre!" It's a very natural, native-sounding phrase.

Maya

Quick word from us: if you want more episodes like this — with transcripts, shadowing practice, and grammar — the Muse English app has it all. Every lesson comes with a podcast, a shadowing story, and quizzes.

Lena

Download it, open today's "Housing and Renting" lesson, and practice these seven words until they feel natural. Your English will get a new lease of life — I promise.

Maya

Welcome back for the final part. Let's review the seven words quickly with examples.

Lena

One — breathe new life into: "The new owners breathed new life into the old café." It means to refresh, to revive.

Maya

Two — get a new lease of life: "After the surgery, my grandfather got a new lease of life." A fresh start, new energy.

Lena

Three — council housing: "They grew up in council housing in Leeds." Public housing owned by the local council.

Maya

Four — council estate: "The new community centre on the council estate opened last week." A neighbourhood of council housing.

Lena

Five — granny flat: "We converted the garage into a granny flat for my mum." A small separate home next to or in the main house.

Maya

Six — furnished: "We rented a furnished flat for the summer." Supplied with furniture.

Lena

Seven — a dog's life: "He worked nights in a freezing warehouse — it was a dog's life." A very hard, miserable life.

Maya

Great. And one more round of the idioms, because they're the trickiest: breathe new life into — to revive something. Get a new lease of life — to start fresh with new energy.

Lena

And remember: nobody deserves to live a dog's life, whether on a council estate or anywhere else. Decent housing matters.

Maya

Exactly. Now go use these words today. Tell someone: "I want to breathe new life into my English." Or: "This lesson gave me a new lease of life."

Lena

Thanks for listening! That was Muse English. See you tomorrow — and until then, happy learning.

📝Grammar: Future in the Past (was about to, was to, would)

Sometimes we talk about the past and need to describe what was **in the future at that time**. English has several forms for this: **would**, **was about to**, **was on the point of**, **was / were to**, and **was to have done**. **Would** + verb is the past of *will*: a prediction, promise, or report seen from the past. *He promised he **would** be back by midnight.* Use it after past reporting verbs: *She said the package **would** arrive the next day.* **Was about to** + verb means something was very close to happening at that time. *I **was about to** leave when the phone rang.* **Was on
