📚 Vocabulary

7 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
abhor /ab-HOR/
verb
To intensely dislike or hate something or someone.
"She truly abhors any form of injustice."
abhorrence /ab-HOR-unss/
noun
A feeling of intense dislike or hatred.
"His abhorrence for violence was evident."
abhorrent /ab-HOR-unt/
adjective
Causing a feeling of intense dislike or hatred.
"Discrimination is abhorrent to a civilized society."
alarmed /uh-LARMD/
adjective
Frightened or worried.
"The sudden noise left her feeling alarmed."
alarming /uh-LAR-ming/
adjective
Causing worry or fear.
"The rise in crime rates is alarming."
altruistic /al-troo-ISS-tik/
adjective
Showing selfless concern for the well-being of others.
"His altruistic actions benefited the entire community."
antipathy /an-TIP-uh-thee/
noun
A deep-seated feeling of dislike or aversion.
"His antipathy towards modern art was well-known."

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome to Muse English! I am Maya, and this is Lena. Today we are talking about feelings, and especially the strong feelings we have about people, ideas, and the world around us.

Lena

That is a great topic, Maya, because our feelings shape so many of our choices. We are going to learn seven powerful words today, and by the end of the episode you will be able to use all of them in real conversations.

Maya

Let us start with a very strong feeling. Imagine you see something unfair happen, and you feel a deep, powerful dislike for it. In English, when that dislike is really intense, we can say that we abhor it. When I say that I abhor cruelty to animals, I mean that I hate it completely, with all my heart.

Lena

That is a strong word, Maya. So, if someone says they abhor lying, they do not just dislike lying a little. They abhor it. They feel that lying is completely wrong.

Maya

Exactly, Lena, and that strength is why we use abhor carefully. We do not say we abhor a rainy day or a boring film. We save abhor for things that feel morally wrong or deeply harmful. When people say they abhor violence, they are making a serious statement about their values, and listeners understand at once how strongly they feel.

Lena

That is a helpful rule. So abhor is not for small annoyances. It is for the big things, the things that truly trouble our conscience. If a friend tells me they abhor cruelty, I know that cruelty goes against everything they believe in, and I respect that honesty.

Maya

And now, before we go further, let me tell you about something that can help you every single day. If you love learning words like this one, the Muse English app is made for you. Every day it gives you fresh words with clear photos and pronunciation, plus this podcast, fun shadowing stories, quick quizzes, grammar lessons, and a leaderboard where you can earn points. It is free to install, so open it today and start building your English one word at a time.

Lena

I love that, Maya. Now back to our feelings. We have the verb abhor, and from it we get a noun. The noun is abhorrence. Abhorrence is the feeling itself, that deep dislike or hatred. For example, a whole community can feel abhorrence for violence, and that shared abhorrence can bring people together to demand change.

Maya

Yes, and abhorrence is a formal word, so you will hear it in speeches and in the news. When a leader speaks with abhorrence about corruption, everyone understands that the feeling is serious and total. It is not a small complaint. Abhorrence means the dislike runs very deep.

Lena

And there is an adjective in the same family, abhorrent. If something is abhorrent, it causes that intense dislike. Many people find discrimination abhorrent, and they say that an abhorrent act has no place in a fair society. When we call something abhorrent, we are judging it very strongly.

Maya

That is right. So we have a family of three words now. We abhor something, we feel abhorrence toward it, and we call it abhorrent. Let me ask you, Lena, is there anything you personally find abhorrent?

Lena

I find cheating in exams abhorrent, Maya. Students work so hard, and cheating feels unfair to everyone. I abhor cheating, and my abhorrence for it has only grown since I became a teacher. What about you?

Maya

I agree completely. I also abhor waste, especially wasting food when so many people are hungry. That kind of waste feels abhorrent to me, and my abhorrence for it is why I always plan my meals carefully.

Lena

Here is another word for dislike, but it is a little different. Antipathy is a deep feeling of dislike or aversion, often one that has lasted a long time. For instance, a person can have an antipathy towards cold weather after growing up in a warm country, and that antipathy never really goes away.

Maya

Antipathy can also be between groups of people, sadly. When two teams have a long rivalry, fans can develop an antipathy for the other side. But antipathy is not always negative in a dramatic way. You can simply have an antipathy towards spicy food, and everyone who knows you will order mild dishes for you.

Lena

That is a good example. Antipathy is quieter than abhorrence, I think. Abhorrence feels like a moral judgment, while antipathy feels more personal, like a settled dislike you carry with you. Do you have any antipathy, Maya?

Maya

I have an antipathy towards alarm clocks, Lena. I know that sounds funny, but that sudden sound in the morning has given me an antipathy that has lasted for years. I still use one, of course, but I do not enjoy it at all.

Lena

That brings us nicely to our next pair of words, which are all about worry. When something frightens you or worries you, you feel alarmed. Imagine you hear a strange noise in the middle of the night. You feel alarmed, and your heart beats faster, because you do not know what caused the sound.

Maya

Feeling alarmed is a natural reaction. A parent feels alarmed when a child comes home late without calling, and a driver feels alarmed when another car suddenly stops ahead. When we feel alarmed, our body is telling us to pay attention and be careful.

Lena

And the thing that causes that feeling is alarming. If the news reports a sudden rise in prices, many families find that alarming, because it affects their daily life. An alarming message from a doctor can leave a patient feeling alarmed for days, until more tests bring good news.

Maya

Let us compare the two words carefully. Alarming describes the cause, and alarmed describes the person. An alarming storm warning leaves coastal towns feeling alarmed, and an alarming letter from the bank can leave anyone feeling alarmed. The two words work together, just like cause and feeling.

Lena

Here is a quick story. Last winter, my neighbour saw smoke near her building and felt alarmed at once. She called for help, and the firefighters said the smoke came from a small kitchen fire. The fire itself was alarming, but everyone was safe, and my neighbour soon stopped feeling alarmed once the firefighters explained everything.

Maya

Let me pause our conversation for a moment to remind you about the Muse English app. If today is your first time hearing these seven words, the app will help you remember them. You can listen to each word on its own, read the shadowing story out loud, test yourself with a short quiz, and study a new grammar point every day. You can also climb the leaderboard and collect points as you learn. It is free, it is fun, and it is waiting for you right now.

Maya

That is a perfect story, Lena. Now let us turn to something much warmer. Our last word today is altruistic, and it is a beautiful word. An altruistic person cares about other people without expecting anything in return. When a stranger donates blood, that is an altruistic act, and it can save a life.

Lena

I love that word, Maya. Altruistic actions are everywhere once you start looking. A teacher who stays late to help a struggling student is being altruistic, and a neighbour who shares food with a family in need is showing an altruistic spirit.

Maya

And here is something important. Being altruistic does not mean being perfect. An altruistic choice can be small, like giving up your seat on a bus, or holding a door for someone carrying heavy bags. Small altruistic moments, repeated every day, can change a whole community.

Lena

That is so true. In fact, researchers say that altruistic behaviour makes the giver happier too. When you do something altruistic, you feel connected to other people, and that connection is good for your own heart as well as theirs.

Maya

Let me ask you something, Lena. Do you think people are born altruistic, or do they learn it?

Lena

I think both are true, Maya. Some children share their toys without being asked, and that looks like a natural altruistic instinct. But families and schools also teach it. When children see adults doing altruistic things, like volunteering at a food bank or helping an elderly neighbour, they learn that caring for others is simply what good people do.

Maya

I agree. And one more thought about our worry words before we finish. When you read an alarming headline, pause and check the facts. Sometimes a headline looks alarming, but the full story is calmer than it first seemed. Feeling alarmed is useful, but it should lead us to think clearly, not to panic.

Lena

That is wise advice. An alarming report deserves attention, not fear. And when someone near you feels alarmed, the kindest thing you can do is stay calm, listen, and help them understand what is really happening. That calm response is itself a small altruistic act.

Maya

Beautifully said, Lena. Our feelings, whether strong dislike or worry or kindness, all connect us to other people, and learning the words for them helps us express ourselves honestly.

Lena

Now let us recap our seven words together. To abhor something is to hate it intensely, and abhorrence is that deep feeling of hatred. Something abhorrent causes that feeling, and an antipathy is a long-lasting dislike. When you feel alarmed, something alarming has worried you, and when you act in an altruistic way, you put other people first.

Maya

Wonderful recap, Lena. Try using these words this week. Tell a friend about something you find abhorrent, describe an alarming headline you read, or thank someone for an altruistic kindness. The more you use a word, the sooner it becomes yours.

Lena

Thanks for listening, everyone. Keep learning, keep feeling, and keep speaking English with confidence. We will see you tomorrow on Muse English.

Maya

Goodbye, and happy learning.

📝Grammar: Inversion After Negative Adverbials

When a sentence starts with a negative or limiting word like **never**, **rarely** or **hardly**, we use question word order: the auxiliary comes before the subject. This pattern is called **inversion**. **Inversion** is formal and emphatic. It is common in writing, speeches and storytelling. If there is no auxiliary, add **do**, **does** or **did**. With **be**, just put **be** first. With **only when**, **only after** and **not until**, the **inversion** happens in the main clause, not in the first part. Remember the pairs: **no sooner** goes with **than**, and **hardly** or **scarcely**
