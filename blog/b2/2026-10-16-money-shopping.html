📚 Vocabulary

7 new words Learn new words with real examples and audio.
overdraft /OH-ver-draft/
noun
spending more money than you have in your bank account
"The bank charged him a fee for going into overdraft again."
cashback /KASH-bak/
noun
money your credit card company gives back to you after you buy something
"Her credit card gives her two percent cashback on every grocery purchase."
markup /MARK-up/
noun
the extra amount a store adds to the price of a product above what it paid for it
"The markup on restaurant wine can be three times the original cost."
mortgage /MOR-gij/
noun
a large loan from a bank to buy a house, paid back over many years
"They finally paid off their mortgage after twenty-five years."
overcharge /oh-ver-CHARJ/
verb
to make someone pay more than something is really worth
"The taxi driver tried to overcharge the tourists for a short ride."
counterfeit /KOWN-ter-fit/
adjective
fake; made to look real, but not real
"The police seized thousands of counterfeit banknotes at the market."
reimburse /ree-im-BURS/
verb
to pay someone back for money they spent, usually for work
"The company will reimburse you for your travel expenses."

📝 Podcast Transcript

Maya

Welcome to Muse English! I'm Maya.

Lena

And I'm Lena. Today we're talking about something that touches every single part of daily life — money and shopping.

Maya

That's right. From bank fees to sneaky price tags, money has its own language. So today we're building your vocabulary with seven money words you'll hear everywhere — in stores, at the bank, and on the news.

Lena

Let's start at the bank. Our first word is overdraft. An overdraft happens when you spend more money than you actually have in your account. The bank lets the payment go through, but it charges you a painful fee for the favor.

Maya

Oh, I have lived this. When I was a student, my rent payment came out one day before my paycheck arrived. I slipped into overdraft, and the bank charged me a thirty-five dollar fee. On top of an already expensive month, that fee really hurt.

Lena

That is exactly how an overdraft works. It saves the payment, but it punishes your wallet. To avoid an overdraft, some people set up a low-balance alert on their banking app. Your phone warns you before your balance hits zero.

Maya

Another trick: link your checking account to your savings account. If you go toward an overdraft, the bank pulls from savings automatically. The fee is much smaller — sometimes there is no fee at all.

Lena

Good advice. An overdraft is one of those quiet money leaks. A few fees a year can quietly cost you more than a nice dinner out.

Maya

Which brings us to our second word, and this one is much happier: cashback. Cashback is money your credit card company gives back to you after you buy something. Spend one hundred dollars, get two dollars back. Simple.

Lena

I genuinely love cashback. My card gives me two percent cashback on groceries and three percent on restaurants. Last year my cashback added up to almost three hundred dollars. That is basically free money for buying things I would buy anyway.

Maya

It really adds up. Some cards give extra cashback in rotating categories — five percent on travel one quarter, five percent on gas the next. If you plan around it, your cashback grows fast.

Lena

But here is the warning, and it is important: cashback only helps if you pay your full bill every month. If you carry a balance and pay interest, the interest wipes out your cashback completely. The banks are counting on exactly that.

Maya

Absolutely. Think of cashback as a small reward, not a strategy. Never buy something just to earn cashback — that is how the reward becomes a trap.

Lena

Wise words. Now let's go shopping. Our third word is markup. A markup is the extra amount a store adds to the price of a product, above what the store itself paid for it.

Maya

The classic example is restaurant wine. A bottle the restaurant bought for ten dollars might be sold to you for thirty dollars. That markup is how the business pays its rent and its staff — but as a customer, it is smart to know it exists.

Lena

Clothing has a big markup too. That simple cotton shirt cost very little to make, but the price tag includes design, advertising, and rent for a beautiful store in a busy mall. Understanding markup helps you decide what is actually worth the price.

Maya

And knowing about markup gives you power. If you know the markup is huge, you can ask for a discount with real confidence. A polite "Can you do any better on the price?" works surprisingly often on big-ticket items like furniture and electronics.

Lena

It really does. Salespeople expect it. A markup is not a crime — it is business — but an informed shopper never pays the first price without thinking.

Maya

Lena

And now, back to money talk. So far we've covered overdraft, cashback, and markup. Next comes the biggest money word in adult life — mortgage. A mortgage is a large loan from a bank that lets you buy a house, which you then pay back over many years, usually twenty or thirty.

Maya

Getting a mortgage is exciting and terrifying at the same time. You finally have your own home, but you also owe the bank hundreds of thousands of dollars. My sister just signed her first mortgage, and she read every single page of the contract before she picked up the pen.

Lena

Very smart. A mortgage has serious fine print: the interest rate, penalties for paying early, insurance requirements, property taxes. Never rush a mortgage decision. Compare at least three banks, and ask each one to explain every fee in plain language.

Maya

Also learn the two main types. A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the same interest rate for the whole term — predictable and calm. A variable-rate mortgage can go up or down, which is cheaper at first but riskier later. Most first-time buyers sleep better with a fixed mortgage.

Lena

And remember the down payment — the chunk of money you pay upfront. A bigger down payment means a smaller mortgage, which means less interest over thirty years. Every extra dollar upfront saves you many dollars later.

Maya

A mortgage is a marathon, not a sprint. Respect it, understand it, and it becomes the path to owning your home instead of renting forever.

Lena

After the mortgage comes the furniture shopping — and that is where our next word bites. To overcharge someone is to make them pay more than something is really worth.

Maya

The classic example is tourists. A taxi driver in a new city might overcharge you for a ten-minute ride if he thinks you do not know the real price. It happens with repairs too — a dishonest mechanic might overcharge you for a simple thirty-minute fix.

Lena

How do you fight it? Check prices first. If three shops sell the same phone for the same price, you will know immediately when the fourth one tries to overcharge you. Knowledge is your shield against any overcharge.

Maya

Ask for an itemized bill, too. When every part and every hour of labor is listed separately, it is much harder for someone to quietly overcharge you. And never feel embarrassed to question a bill — it is your money.

Lena

Exactly. An overcharge survives on silence. The moment you ask questions, it usually disappears.

Maya

Speaking of traps — our sixth word is counterfeit. Counterfeit means fake: money or products made to look real, but they are not.

Lena

Counterfeit bills are a real problem. A careful cashier holds your fifty-dollar note up to the light, checking for the watermark and the security strip. If the bill is counterfeit, the store loses the money — the police take the fake bill, and nobody pays you back.

Maya

Modern bills have clever defenses: raised ink you can feel with your finger, color-shifting numbers, tiny hidden text. Take ten seconds to learn what your country's real money looks and feels like. A counterfeit bill often feels too smooth or too perfect.

Lena

And it is not just money. Counterfeit sneakers, counterfeit perfumes, counterfeit phone chargers — online marketplaces are full of them. A charger that costs two dollars might be counterfeit, and a counterfeit charger can overheat and actually start a fire.

Maya

That is genuinely scary. Buy electronics from trusted sellers only. If a deal looks impossible — a famous brand at one tenth of the price — it is almost certainly counterfeit. Walk away.

Lena

Maya

Welcome back for our final word: reimburse. To reimburse someone is to pay them back for money they spent — usually for work.

Lena

The perfect example is business trips. You pay for the flight, the hotel, the meals — and then the company reimburses you. Keep every single receipt, because no receipt usually means no reimbursement.

Maya

My company is good about it — they reimburse travel costs within a week. My friend's company takes two months to reimburse anything. She basically gives them an interest-free loan every single trip, and she hates it.

Lena

Two months! Here is a pro tip: photograph your receipts the same day. Paper fades, phones get lost, but a photo in the cloud means the company can always reimburse you on time.

Maya

And know your company's policy before you spend. Some companies reimburse business-class flights; others reimburse only economy. Spend first and ask later, and you might pay the difference yourself.

Lena

So here is our full set. Overdraft — spending past zero and paying a fee. Cashback — money your card gives back to you. Markup — the store's profit built into the price. Mortgage — the big home loan you pay for decades. Overcharge — being made to pay too much. Counterfeit — dangerously fake. And reimburse — getting paid back for what you spent.

Maya

Seven words, one smarter wallet. This week, check your bank app for an overdraft alert, look at your cashback balance, question a markup before you pay it, and never let anyone overcharge you.

Lena

If a deal smells counterfeit, walk away. If you spent money for work, make them reimburse you. That is money English in action.

Maya

Thanks for learning with us today. Keep practicing, keep listening, and we will see you tomorrow on Muse English!

Lena

Bye for now!

📝Grammar: Third Conditional

We use the third conditional to imagine a different past. It talks about things that **did not happen**, and the results that **would have followed**. The structure is **If + past perfect** in the condition, and **would have + past participle** in the result. Example: **If I had known** about the markup, **I would have asked** for a discount. We often use it for regrets about money and shopping. **If she had checked** her balance, the bank **would not have charged** her an overdraft fee. Both clauses can be negative. **If he had not lost** his receipts, the company **would not have refused*
