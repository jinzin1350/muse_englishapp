Much, Many, A Lot Of, A Few, A Little

Level: A2 · Free grammar guide

H1: Much, Many, A Lot Of, A Few, A Little

Direct answer

Use many with countable nouns (things you can count: books, friends, apples). Use much with uncountable nouns (things you cannot count: water, time, money). Use a lot of with both. Use a few (countable) and a little (uncountable) for small amounts. Few and little without a mean "not enough" — a negative idea.

The rule / form box

Countable nouns — you can count them: one apple, two apples, three books

  • many — How many apples do you want?
  • a few — I have a few friends in Toronto. (a small number, but enough)
  • few — I have few friends here. (not enough — negative)
  • a lot of — She has a lot of books.

Uncountable nouns — you cannot count them: water, rice, money, time, information

  • much — How much water do you drink?
  • a little — I have a little money left. (a small amount, but enough)
  • little — I have little money left. (not enough — negative)
  • a lot of — He has a lot of time.

Much in positive sentences: In positive sentences, a lot of is the normal choice: I have a lot of time. Much on its own sounds unnatural there with most nouns (❌ He has much money). Much is natural in positive sentences after so, too and as (He has so much money. I ate too much.) and in formal English (Much has been written about this.).

Note: Few and little on their own sound formal. In everyday speech, people usually say not many / not much: I don't have many friends here.

A few vs Few / A little vs Little:

  • A few = some, enough. I have a few eggs — I can make an omelette.
  • Few = almost none, not enough. I have few eggs — I can't make an omelette.
  • A little = some, enough. I have a little time — let's have coffee.
  • Little = almost none, not enough. I have little time — I can't stop.

Examples (10)

  1. How many people are coming to dinner? (Countable — people.)
  2. How much sugar do you take in your tea? (Uncountable — sugar.)
  3. I don't have many photos from that trip. (Countable, negative.)
  4. I don't have much free time this week. (Uncountable, negative.)
  5. She has a lot of experience. (Uncountable — experience. A lot of works with both.)
  6. There are a lot of cars on the road today. (Countable — cars.)
  7. I have a few minutes before the meeting. (Countable — minutes, enough.)
  8. I have a little money saved for the trip. (Uncountable — money, enough.)
  9. He has few friends in this city, so he feels lonely. (Countable — not enough, negative.)
  10. We have little time left — let's hurry. (Uncountable — not enough, negative.)

    11. Common mistakes

    ❌ How much apples do you want?

    ✅ How many apples do you want? (Apples are countable → many.)

    ❌ How many water do you drink?

    ✅ How much water do you drink? (Water is uncountable → much.)

    ❌ I have a few money.

    ✅ I have a little money. (Money is uncountable → a little, not a few.)

    ❌ I have little friends here.

    ✅ I have few friends here. / I have a few friends here. (Few for countable, little for uncountable. Few friends = not enough. A few friends = some, enough. I have little friends would mean small or young friends.)

    ❌ There are much people here.

    ✅ There are many people here. / There are a lot of people here. (People are countable → many or a lot of.)

    Exercises

    Choose the correct answer. You will see the explanation after checking.

    1. How ___ apples do you need?
    Apples are countable → *many*.
    2. How ___ water do you drink every day?
    Water is uncountable → *much*.
    3. I don't have ___ time today.
    Time is uncountable → *much*. In a negative sentence, *much* is natural.
    4. She has ___ friends in this town.
    Friends are countable, small amount (enough) → *a few*.
    5. I have ___ money left, but enough for a coffee.
    Money is uncountable, small amount but enough → *a little*.
    6. There are ___ cars on the road this morning.
    Cars are countable. *A lot of* works with countable nouns. (*Many* would also be correct, but it is not an option.)
    7. He has ___ experience — this is his first job.
    Experience is uncountable. *Little* = almost none, not enough (negative). This is his first job, so he has almost no experience.
    8. I have ___ eggs — I can make an omelette.
    Eggs are countable. *A few* = some, enough to make an omelette.
    9. We have very ___ time left — we need to hurry.
    *Very little* = almost none.
    10. How ___ information do you need?
    Information is uncountable → *much*.
    11. Choose the correct sentence:
    People are countable → *How many...?*
    12. I have ___ good ideas for the project.
    Ideas are countable, small amount (enough) → *a few*.
    13. She has ___ patience with slow computers.
    Patience is uncountable. *Little* = almost none, not enough.
    14. There isn't ___ food in the fridge.
    Food (as a general substance) is uncountable → *much*. In a negative sentence, *much* is natural.
    15. I have ___ minutes before my train leaves.
    Minutes are countable, small amount (enough) → *a few*.
    16. He has ___ money, so he can buy anything he wants.
    *A lot of* is used with uncountable nouns for a large amount. *Much* on its own sounds unnatural here (*He has much money* ✗). You could say: *He has so much money.*
